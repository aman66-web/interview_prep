# Lanebrook Swim Schools: is the seller's plan believable?

Case type: m_and_a · Industry: Children's swim schools · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 35 minutes · Style: candidate_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/lanebrook-swim-schools-due-diligence

## 1. What the candidate is told

Mosswick Capital, a UK private equity fund, is bidding to buy Lanebrook Swim Schools, a chain that teaches children aged 3 to 11 to swim at 60 sites across the Midlands and the north of England. Lanebrook's revenue is £18 million this year. The seller's business plan says it will double to £36 million in five years, and Mosswick's bid is based on that plan. Mosswick has asked us to carry out the commercial due diligence before final bids are due in four weeks: is the market growing, does Lanebrook keep its customers, and is the plan believable? In the end, Mosswick wants to know what year-5 revenue it should believe. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The deal.** Lanebrook's two founders want to retire and are selling the whole business through an auction run by their adviser. Final bids are due in four weeks. Mosswick's current offer is based on the seller's plan, and it would expect to own Lanebrook for about five years before selling it on.

**Our scope and Mosswick's question.** We are doing the commercial due diligence only: the market, customers, competitors and the revenue plan. Other advisers check the accounts, tax and legal matters, and Mosswick does the valuation itself. Mosswick wants a year-5 revenue figure it can believe (its own base case), what could make it higher or lower, and anything that should stop the deal.

**How the lessons work.** Children aged 3 to 11 have one 30-minute lesson a week in a group of up to 6, with the same teacher each week. Lanebrook rents pool time by the hour from schools, hotels and leisure centres and owns no pools. It employs its own swimming teachers. Most children join at 4 or 5.

**Customers and fees.** About 30,000 children are enrolled, roughly 500 per site, and that number has been flat for three years: sign-ups have roughly matched leavers. Parents pay a fixed monthly fee of £50 by direct debit and can cancel with one month's notice. All revenue comes from these fees: 30,000 children x £50 x 12 months = £18m. The plan's year-5 revenue of £36m is counted the same way, as a run rate: children enrolled at the end of year 5 x the year-5 monthly fee x 12.

**Price history.** Lanebrook raised its fee by £2 a month in each of the last three years, from £44 to £50, and the share of children leaving each month did not change. The plan keeps raising it by £2 a year, to £60 in year 5.

**Competitors.** In Lanebrook's areas, council-run leisure centres charge about £35 to £40 a month for bigger groups and often have waiting lists. Two other private chains charge £48 to £58, and many independent teachers run lessons at a single pool. Parents tell Lanebrook they choose it for the small groups and having the same teacher every week.

**Children leaving.** About 4% of enrolled children leave each month (4.1%, 4.0% and 4.0% in the last three years), so a child stays about two years on average. Lanebrook asks every leaving parent for one main reason, and about half answer.

**The parent app.** Last year Lanebrook built a parent app with progress badges and easy lesson swaps, and tested it at 5 sites for six months. The leaving rate at those sites fell from 4.0% to 3.6% a month; at the other sites it stayed at 4.0%. The plan rolls the app out to every site in year 1.

**Capacity at existing sites.** Lessons run in after-school and weekend slots, which are about 90% full at a typical site, so a site could take only about 555 children without renting more pool hours. Pool owners rarely offer extra hours at those times. Weekday daytime pool hours are easy to rent, but few children are free then, apart from those not yet at school.

**Past openings.** Lanebrook opened 5 sites a year for four years, between eight and eleven years ago (20 of its 60 sites), and never more than 5 in one year; the other 40 sites are older. It has opened none since, because the founders chose to focus on the sites they had. Its two-person property team says it can find about 5 suitable pools a year; the plan's 7 a year would need a bigger team.

**Teachers.** Lanebrook has about 700 part-time swimming teachers. Hiring has kept up so far, but it has never had to staff more than 5 new sites in one year.

**The seller's view of the market.** The seller's adviser says the number of children taking paid swimming lessons in Lanebrook's areas will grow by about 10% over five years, because more young children will live there.

**Our market research.** Our team surveyed 2,000 parents in the areas within 20 minutes' drive of Lanebrook's existing and planned sites, and used local population forecasts for those areas. It covers paid weekly lessons from every kind of provider. The year-5 share taking lessons comes from the survey: about 30% of parents of children under 3 say they plan to pay for weekly lessons once their child is old enough, against the 25% of children aged 3 to 11 who have them today. These are parents' stated plans, not a past trend, and the forecast assumes they follow through.

**Profit.** Lanebrook's EBITDA (profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) is about £3.6m this year, 20% of revenue, and the plan keeps that margin. Pool hire and teachers' pay are the largest costs. The financial advisers check costs and the margin, so they are outside our work.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Market: children taking paid lessons in Lanebrook's areas, split into the number of children and the share who take lessons, plus price
- Customers and retention: how long children stay, why they leave, and whether the plan's lower leaving rate is achievable
- Competition and capacity: Lanebrook's price and offer against council and private rivals, and how full existing sites are
- The plan: year-5 revenue split into existing sites, new sites (how many open and how fast they fill) and the fee, each tested against evidence
- So what for the deal: a base-case revenue Mosswick can believe, the upside and downside, and any risk that should stop the deal

Good signs:
- Frames the job as testing the seller's plan one assumption at a time, against the three questions Mosswick asked
- Builds revenue as sites x children per site x fee, and splits the growth into its sources
- Asks for Lanebrook's own history (past openings, how fast new sites filled, past leaving rates and price rises) to test each assumption
- States a hypothesis early, for example that new sites carry most of the growth and are the least proven part
- Asks about capacity at existing sites and whether pools and teachers can be found for new ones

Red flags:
- Uses a generic market-entry or profitability framework without tying it to the plan's assumptions
- Accepts the seller's plan as given, or dismisses all of it without testing each assumption
- Ignores that new sites take years to fill
- Spends the time on valuation, financing or the accounts, which are outside the scope

## 4. Exhibits (shown when the candidate asks for the right data)

### Seller's business plan: key assumptions

Shown when the candidate: Ask to see the seller's business plan or its key assumptions.

| Assumption | This year | Year 5 (plan) | Reason given in the plan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sites | 60 | 95 | 7 new sites a year |
| Children per existing site | 500 | 600 | Market growth of about 10% plus children staying longer |
| Children per new site | None yet | 400 | What Lanebrook's earlier openings reached |
| Monthly fee (£) | 50 | 60 | £2 rise each year, as in the last three years |
| Children leaving each month (%) | 4 | 2.5 | New parent app and progress badges |
| Children enrolled (thousand) | 30 | 50 | Existing sites plus new sites |
| Revenue (£ million) | 18 | 36 | Children enrolled at the end of year 5 x monthly fee x 12 |

What the data says: The plan doubles revenue from £18m to £36m. Of the 20,000 extra children, 14,000 come from 35 new sites (each counted at 400) and 6,000 from existing sites (500 to 600 each). Of the £18m of growth, about £10m comes from new sites, about £4.3m from more children at existing sites and £3.6m from the fee rise on today's children. It needs 67% more children (30,000 to 50,000) after three flat years. New sites are the biggest and least proven part: the plan counts every new site at 400 children in year 5, even one opened only months earlier, and assumes 7 openings a year. The fall in the leaving rate from 4% to 2.5% is its boldest assumption about customers.

### Children's swimming lessons in Lanebrook's areas: our research

Shown when the candidate: Ask about the size or growth of the market in Lanebrook's areas, or for our own market research.

| Measure | This year | Year 5 forecast |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Children aged 3 to 11 (thousand) | 1200 | 1100 |
| Share taking paid weekly swimming lessons (%) | 25 | 30 |

What the data says: Children taking paid lessons grow from 300,000 (1,200k x 25%) to 330,000 (1,100k x 30%): +10% in five years, about 2% a year. That matches the plan's 10%, so the market assumption holds on our numbers, but for a different reason from the one the seller gives: the number of children falls by about 8%, and all the growth comes from a larger share of children taking lessons. So growth depends on more families starting paid lessons (a forecast from what parents say they plan), not on more children, which makes it the softest of the assumptions that hold. Lanebrook teaches about 10% of these children today (30,000 of 300,000). The plan's 50,000 children would be about 15% of 330,000: Lanebrook would take two-thirds of the market's growth (20,000 of the 30,000 extra children).

### Main reason parents gave for leaving Lanebrook, last 12 months (share of leavers who answered, %)

Shown when the candidate: Ask why children leave, or for data on retention or churn.

| Main reason given | Share of leavers (%) |
| --- | --- |
| My child can now swim confidently | 40 |
| Lesson times no longer suit us | 20 |
| Cost | 15 |
| Moved home or other reasons | 15 |
| Unhappy with the lessons or the teacher | 10 |
| Total | 100 |

What the data says: 40% of leavers go because their child has learned to swim: a sign the lessons work, and not something an app can prevent. Unhappiness with the lessons (10%) is within Lanebrook's control, and lesson times (20%) partly are, through easy lesson swaps, though peak slots are nearly full. Cost is left out because the plan raises the fee every year, so it cannot also win back parents who leave over price. Even if Lanebrook kept every child who leaves over lesson times or unhappiness, the leaving rate would fall from 4% to 2.8% a month (the best case without cutting prices), still above the plan's 2.5%; the app pilot reached 3.6%. Today a child stays about 25 months on average (1 / 4%); the plan implies 40 months (1 / 2.5%). Only about half of leaving parents gave a reason, so these shares may not hold for every leaver.

| Years since opening | Children enrolled per site |
| --- | --- |
| End of year 1 | 120 |
| End of year 2 | 220 |
| End of year 3 | 300 |
| End of year 4 | 360 |

What the data says: New sites take five years to reach 400 children; these older openings are at 500 today. The plan counts every new site at 400 in year 5, but only sites opened in year 1 will have had five years. With openings at the start of each year, one site opened in each of the five years gives five sites holding 400 + 360 + 300 + 220 + 120 = 1,400 children at the end of year 5, or 280 a site on average, not 400. The data come from openings eight to eleven years ago, when there may have been fewer competitors, so even this pace could be optimistic.

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** Using our research, by what percentage will the number of children taking paid weekly swimming lessons in Lanebrook's areas grow over the next five years?

Inputs: children_today = 1200, share_today = 25, children_y5 = 1100, share_y5 = 30
Expected answer: 10 (accepted within 5%)
Working: Today 1,200k x 25% = 300k children take lessons. In year 5, 1,100k x 30% = 330k. 330 / 300 = 1.10, so +10% over five years (about 2% a year). That matches the plan's 10%, but the growth comes from a higher share taking lessons, which rests on what parents say they plan, while the number of children falls by about 8%.

**Question 2.** Suppose Lanebrook kept every child who now leaves because lesson times no longer suit or because the family is unhappy with the lessons. What would the monthly leaving rate be, in percent?

Inputs: leaving_rate = 4, times_pct = 20, unhappy_pct = 10
Expected answer: 2.8 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Those two reasons are 20% + 10% = 30% of leavers. 4% x (1 - 0.30) = 2.8% a month, still above the plan's 2.5%. Reaching 2.5% means cutting leavers by 37.5% (1 - 2.5/4): all of the lesson-times and unhappy leavers plus half of those who leave over cost, while the plan raises the fee by £2 every year, or keeping children who can already swim.

**Question 3.** If Lanebrook opens 7 new sites at the start of each of the next five years, as the plan says, and each fills at the same pace as its earlier openings, how many children will the 35 new sites have at the end of year 5?

Inputs: openings_per_year = 7, y1 = 120, y2 = 220, y3 = 300, y4 = 360, y5 = 400
Expected answer: 9800 (accepted within 2%)
Working: At the end of year 5, the 7 sites opened in year 1 have had five years (400 each), year-2 sites four years (360), year-3 sites three (300), year-4 sites two (220) and year-5 sites one (120). 400 + 360 + 300 + 220 + 120 = 1,400; x 7 = 9,800 children, against 14,000 (35 x 400) in the plan.

**Question 4.** For a base case, assume 5 new sites a year, opening at the start of each year (the pace Lanebrook kept up for four years when it last opened sites, and what its property team expects), each filling at the same pace as before; children per existing site growing in line with the market (+10%); and the plan's £60 monthly fee. What is Lanebrook's annual revenue run rate at the end of year 5, on the same basis as the plan (children enrolled at the end of year 5 x the monthly fee x 12), in £ million?

Inputs: existing_sites = 60, children_per_site = 500, market_growth_pct = 10, openings_per_year = 5, y1 = 120, y2 = 220, y3 = 300, y4 = 360, y5 = 400, fee_year5 = 60
Expected answer: 28.8 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Existing sites: 60 x 500 x 1.1 = 33,000 children (550 a site, which leaves existing sites effectively full against peak capacity of about 555, so this is the top of the range without more pool hours). New sites: 5 x 1,400 = 7,000. Total 40,000 children x £60 x 12 = £28.8m: 20% below the plan's £36m but 60% above today's £18m. The £7.2m gap is about £5m from new sites (7,000 fewer children) and about £2.2m from existing sites (3,000 fewer).

## 6. Brainstorm

Suppose Mosswick buys Lanebrook. What could it do over its five years of ownership to grow revenue above our base case?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- Keep children who can already swim with follow-on classes such as stroke improvement, junior squads or lifesaving, since 40% of leavers go because their child can now swim
- Run weekday daytime lessons for children not yet at school, using pool hours that are easy to rent
- Add parent-and-baby or toddler classes for under-3s in daytime slots
- Offer adult lessons in off-peak hours
- Run intensive courses in the school holidays
- Sell daytime lessons to primary schools for their pupils
- Rent more pool hours or lease a few dedicated teaching pools in the fullest areas, to lift capacity above about 555 children a site
- Use the app's lesson swaps to fill the last places in peak slots and keep children who leave over lesson times
- Buy small independent swim schools to add sites and children faster than opening new ones
- Grow the property team and train more teachers so that openings can safely rise above 5 a year
- Sibling or pay-yearly discounts to cut leaving over cost, weighed against the planned fee rises
- A premium tier with smaller groups at a higher fee
- Better teacher training and support to cut the 10% who leave unhappy with the lessons

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- The plan's £36m is not believable; a base case of about £28.8m in year 5 is, about 20% below the plan but still 60% above today's £18m
- New sites are the biggest gap: the plan counts every new site at 400 children and assumes 7 openings a year, but sites take five years to reach 400 and Lanebrook has never opened more than 5 in a year
- The market (+10%) and price (£2 a year) assumptions hold, but the retention assumption does not: even keeping every child who leaves over lesson times or unhappiness gives 2.8%, so 2.5% would also need half of the cost leavers kept while fees rise each year; the app pilot reached 3.6%

Good signs:
- Good versus better: a good answer says the plan is too optimistic; a better one sizes the gap (about £5m from new sites, about £2.2m from existing sites), says which assumptions hold, and turns it into deal terms: base the returns model and the bid on about £29m of year-5 revenue (about £5.8m EBITDA at the plan's 20% margin) rather than £36m (£7.2m EBITDA), and treat the plan as upside or tie part of the price to how new sites perform
- Points out that market growth comes only from a rising share of children taking lessons while the number of children falls, so growth depends on more families starting paid lessons (a forecast from what parents say they plan), not on more children
- Checks the plan against market share: 50,000 of 330,000 children is about 15%, up from 10% today, so Lanebrook would take two-thirds of the market's growth; the base case's 40,000 is about 12%
- Uses capacity: peak slots are about 90% full (about 555 children a site), so the plan's 600 at existing sites fails on capacity whatever the leaving rate; even the pilot's 3.6% would push demand above it, so better retention does not change the base case without more pool hours
- Questions the evidence: only about half of leaving parents gave a reason, so the reason shares may not hold for every leaver, and a six-month pilot at 5 sites is a small test of the app
- Names risks and next steps: fee rises against cheaper council lessons, finding pools and teachers for new sites, and ramp-up data from openings eight to eleven years ago; next steps such as calls with pool owners and a check of competitors' opening plans

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: Mosswick needs a year-5 revenue it can believe and the main risks, and our scope is commercial (market, customers, competitors, the plan), not the accounts or the valuation. Revenue is children x fee: 60 sites x 500 children x £50 x 12 = £18m, and the plan's £36m is a run rate on the same basis at the end of year 5.
2. Structure: split the plan's £36m into its drivers (children at existing sites, new sites and how fast they fill, the fee) and test each against our market research, Lanebrook's own history and site capacity. Retention feeds into children per site. Hypothesis: new sites carry most of the growth and are the least proven.
3. The plan: of the £18m of growth, about £10m comes from 35 new sites counted at 400 children each (14,000 children), about £4.3m from 100 more children at each existing site and £3.6m from the fee rising to £60. It needs 67% more children (30,000 to 50,000) after three flat years, which is why the new-site and existing-site assumptions need the most scrutiny.
4. Market and price hold: children taking lessons in Lanebrook's areas go from 300,000 to 330,000, +10% in five years as the plan says, but because the share taking lessons rises from 25% to 30% while the number of children falls from 1.2m to 1.1m. That rise rests on what parents say they plan, not a past trend, so it is the softest of the assumptions that hold. The plan would also lift Lanebrook's share of the market from 10% to about 15%. Fee rises of £2 a year have not raised the leaving rate for three years, so £60 is believable, though it is above the £58 top of rivals' prices today.
5. Retention does not hold: the leaving rate has been 4% a month for three years (about 25 months' average stay). 40% of leavers have learned to swim; keeping every child who leaves over lesson times or unhappiness gives 2.8%, and 2.5% would also need half of those who leave over cost kept while the fee rises each year; the app pilot reached 3.6% at 5 sites. In any case capacity, not retention, limits existing sites: peak slots are about 90% full (about 555 children a site). About 22 children a site would join each month (4% x 500, plus the market's 10%), so even the pilot's 3.6% would settle at about 610 a site, above capacity. Existing sites can reach about 550 (+10% with the market), which leaves them effectively full, not 600.
6. New sites do not hold: sites take five years to reach 400 children, so even at 7 openings a year the new sites hold 9,800 children in year 5, not 14,000. Lanebrook has never opened more than 5 a year (it kept up 5 a year for four years when it last opened sites) and its property team expects about 5, which gives 7,000.
7. Base case: (33,000 + 7,000) children x £60 x 12 = £28.8m at the end of year 5, 20% below the plan but 60% above today (about 10% a year). The £7.2m gap is about £5m from new sites and about £2.2m from existing sites. Downsides: if fees reach only £55, revenue is £26.4m; at 4 openings a year, new sites hold 5,600 children and revenue is (33,000 + 5,600) x £720 = about £27.8m. Upside: follow-on classes, daytime lessons for pre-school children, more pool hours and buying small swim schools.
8. Recommendation: nothing found should stop the deal: it is a stable business whose fee rises have stuck, in a market that holds up on our numbers. But Mosswick should base its returns model and its bid on about £29m of year-5 revenue, about £5.8m EBITDA at the plan's 20% margin (which the financial advisers are checking), rather than £36m (£7.2m EBITDA), and treat the plan as upside or tie part of the price to new-site results. Next steps before the bid: calls with pool owners about extra hours and new sites, a check of rivals' opening plans, and the app's results as it rolls out.

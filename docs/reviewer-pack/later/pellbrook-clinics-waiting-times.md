# Pellbrook Clinics: why are waits getting longer?

Case type: operations · Industry: Healthcare · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 25 minutes · Style: interviewer_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/pellbrook-clinics-waiting-times

## 1. What the candidate is told

Pellbrook Clinics runs 8 private outpatient clinics in the English Midlands, where patients see senior specialist doctors (consultants). A year ago, a new patient waited about 2 weeks for a first appointment. Today the wait is about 6 weeks, and patients are starting to complain. The CEO has asked you to find out why waits have grown and how to bring them back down. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The company.** Pellbrook runs 8 private outpatient clinics in the English Midlands. Consultants see patients in five specialties, mainly orthopaedics, dermatology, and ear, nose and throat. This case is about first appointments only; follow-up appointments run in separate sessions.

**Objective.** The CEO wants the average wait for a first appointment back to 2 weeks within 6 months, without opening new clinics or raising prices.

**Patients, payment and insurers.** About 60% of patients are covered by two health insurers; the other 40% pay for themselves. A first appointment earns Pellbrook £200 either way. Both insurers have warned that they will start sending patients to other providers if waits stay above 3 weeks.

**Consultants and sessions.** Consultants run 100 first-appointment sessions a week across the group, each with 10 slots. That has not changed in the last year. Consultants are paid a fixed £1,000 per session whether or not every slot is filled. Most also work in the NHS on weekdays, so extra weekday sessions are hard to staff, but enough have offered Saturday work to run up to 25 Saturday sessions a week across the group.

**Rooms and costs of extra sessions.** Rooms are not a constraint: the clinics are closed on Saturdays and about a quarter of weekday room time is unused. A Saturday session has 10 slots, like a weekday one, and costs about £1,500 to run, including the consultant's higher Saturday rate and opening the building.

**Demand.** Requests for first appointments from new patients have been roughly flat over the last year, and the mix across specialties has barely changed. Marketing has not changed. Waits vary by specialty and clinic, from about 3 to 9 weeks; urgent referrals are fitted in within 2 weeks.

**Booking, reminders and cancelling.** Patients book online or by phone and get one confirmation email. A year ago, to save money, Pellbrook stopped the reminder phone calls it used to make two days before each appointment. Cancelling or rebooking needs a phone call during office hours. Pellbrook does not charge for missed or late-cancelled appointments.

**Missed appointments.** A slot is wasted when a patient does not turn up or cancels less than 48 hours ahead. These slots almost always stay empty, because Pellbrook has no list of patients who could come in at short notice. Nearly all of these patients still need to be seen, so they rebook and take the next free slot, weeks later. Empty slots have crept up month by month since the reminder calls stopped.

**Why patients miss appointments.** In a recent survey of patients who missed a first appointment or cancelled late, most said they had forgotten an appointment booked weeks earlier, or that the date no longer suited them and they could not get through on the phone to change it in time. A few had already been seen somewhere else.

**The waiting list.** The waiting list counts every patient who has asked for a first appointment and not yet been seen, including those rebooking after a missed slot. Nobody has checked it for over a year to see whether everyone on it still needs an appointment.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Demand: requests for first appointments and how they have changed
- Capacity on paper: consultant sessions, slots per session and rooms
- Capacity actually used: no-shows, late cancellations and empty slots, and whether those patients rebook
- The booking process: reminders, cancelling and filling gaps at short notice
- A plan to clear the backlog and keep waits down, with its cost and timing

Good signs:
- Treats the waiting list as a queue: waits grow when fewer patients are seen than ask for an appointment
- Checks demand and capacity on paper before proposing new capacity
- Asks how many slots are actually used, not just how many are offered
- Asks whether patients who miss an appointment rebook, since that is why each empty slot adds to the list
- Separates two jobs: stop the list growing, then shrink the backlog

Red flags:
- Jumps to hiring consultants or opening clinics without checking how existing slots are used
- Assumes demand must have risen without asking for the data
- Counts the 200 empty slots a week as the list's weekly growth, instead of comparing patients seen (800) with new requests (900)
- Ignores the link between long waits and missed appointments

## 4. Exhibits (shown when the candidate asks for the right data)

### First appointments across the group: an average week, and the waiting list

Shown when the candidate: Ask for demand and capacity for first appointments: requests, slots offered, how many slots are used and the size of the waiting list, now versus a year ago.

| Measure | A year ago | Last month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New patients asking for a first appointment (a week) | 900 | 900 |
| Slots offered (a week) | 1000 | 1000 |
| Empty slots: no-shows and late cancellations (a week) | 100 | 200 |
| Patients seen (a week) | 900 | 800 |
| Patients on the waiting list | 1800 | 4800 |

What the data says: Requests and slots have not changed, but empty slots doubled from 100 to 200 a week (10% to 20% of slots). Patients who miss rebook, so they stay on the list: Pellbrook now sees 100 fewer patients a week than ask for an appointment, and the waiting list grew from 1,800 to 4,800, from 2 weeks to 6 weeks of patients seen.

### Empty slots last month, by how far ahead the appointment was booked (average week)

Shown when the candidate: Ask which appointments end up empty, or whether missed appointments depend on how long the patient waited.

| Booked ahead | Slots | Empty slots | Empty-slot rate (%) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Less than 2 weeks | 100 | 5 | 5 |
| 2 to 4 weeks | 300 | 45 | 15 |
| More than 4 weeks | 600 | 150 | 25 |
| All slots | 1000 | 200 | 20 |

What the data says: The longer a patient waits, the more likely the slot is wasted: 5% of slots booked under 2 weeks ahead are empty, against 25% of those booked more than 4 weeks ahead. Long-wait bookings cause 150 of the 200 empty slots (75%). Long waits create empty slots, and because those patients rebook, empty slots make waits longer: a vicious circle. It also runs the other way: shorter waits mean fewer missed appointments.

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** By how many patients a week is the waiting list growing now?

Inputs: requests = 900, slots = 1000, empty = 200
Expected answer: 100 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Slots used = 1,000 - 200 = 800 patients seen a week. 900 new patients ask for an appointment, and those who miss rebook and stay on the list, so the list grows by 900 - 800 = 100 patients a week.

**Question 2.** Each first appointment earns £200, and with thousands of patients waiting, every empty slot is an appointment Pellbrook could have filled. How much revenue a year do the empty slots cost, in £ million? Assume 50 working weeks a year.

Inputs: empty = 200, fee = 200, weeks = 50
Expected answer: 2 (accepted within 3%)
Working: 200 empty slots x £200 = £40,000 a week. Over 50 weeks that is £2,000,000, or £2 million a year. Consultants are paid per session anyway, so almost all of it is lost profit.

**Question 3.** What percentage of empty slots comes from appointments booked more than 4 weeks ahead?

Inputs: empty_long = 150, empty_all = 200
Expected answer: 75 (accepted within 2%)
Working: 150 of the 200 empty slots a week were booked more than 4 weeks ahead. 150 / 200 = 75%.

**Question 4.** Suppose reminders and filling late gaps bring empty slots back to 100 a week, so Pellbrook again sees 900 patients a week, the same number as ask for one. It also runs 25 Saturday sessions a week, each seeing 10 patients from the waiting list. How many weeks until the list is down to a 2-week wait?

Inputs: waiting = 4800, target_weeks = 2, seen_weekly = 900, saturday_sessions = 25, slots_per_session = 10
Expected answer: 12 (accepted within 4%)
Working: A 2-week wait at 900 patients a week means a list of 2 x 900 = 1,800. The list must shrink by 4,800 - 1,800 = 3,000. Weekday clinics now keep pace with requests, so only the Saturday clinics shrink the list: 25 x 10 = 250 patients a week, and 3,000 / 250 = 12 weeks, about 3 months. The list falls below the insurers' 3-week line (2,700 patients) after about 8 to 9 weeks.

## 6. Brainstorm

What could Pellbrook do to cut the number of empty slots, and to fill the ones that still come up?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- Bring back reminders, for example a text a week before and two days before each appointment
- Let patients cancel or rebook with one tap from the reminder, at any time of day
- Keep a short-notice list of patients who can come in at a day's notice, and offer them freed-up slots
- Contact everyone on the waiting list to check they still need an appointment, and remove those who do not
- Book one or two extra patients into sessions where empty slots are common
- Ask self-pay patients for a deposit, or charge a fee for late cancellations
- Ask patients to confirm a week before, and release unconfirmed slots to the short-notice list
- Offer video first appointments where the consultant agrees they are suitable, since they are easier to keep
- Run extra sessions to clear the backlog so that new bookings are only a few weeks ahead
- Track empty slots by clinic and specialty every week and act on the worst

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Requests and slots are unchanged; the list grows because empty slots doubled from 100 to 200 a week and those patients rebook, so 100 fewer patients are seen each week than ask for an appointment
- Most empty slots (75%) are appointments booked more than 4 weeks ahead: long waits cause missed appointments, which make waits longer
- A clear plan: cut empty slots with reminders, easy cancelling and short-notice filling, plus temporary Saturday clinics that clear the backlog in about 12 weeks, rather than new clinics

Good signs:
- Puts a value on the problem: empty slots cost about £2m of revenue a year
- Sees that the insurers' warning is the bigger risk: their patients bring 60% of first-appointment revenue, about £5m a year
- Checks that Saturday sessions pay for themselves: about £2,000 of fees against about £1,500 of cost per session, so they still cover their cost with two empty slots
- Suggests checking the waiting list, since some people on it may already have been seen elsewhere
- Names what to track each week: the empty-slot rate, the size of the list and the wait, against the insurers' 3-week line

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: first-appointment waits have gone from 2 to 6 weeks; the goal is 2 weeks within 6 months with no new clinics or price rises; both insurers (60% of patients) will move patients if waits stay above 3 weeks.
2. Structure: treat the waiting list as a queue. Compare demand (new requests) with capacity actually used (slots offered minus empty slots), then look at why slots go empty and how to clear the backlog.
3. Analysis: requests (900 a week) and slots (1,000) are unchanged, but empty slots doubled to 200, so only 800 patients are seen. Patients who miss rebook, so the list grows by 100 a week. It has grown from 1,800 to 4,800, which is 6 weeks of patients seen.
4. Root cause: reminder calls stopped a year ago, cancelling needs a phone call in office hours and freed slots are never refilled. 25% of slots booked over 4 weeks ahead go empty, against 5% of those booked under 2 weeks ahead, and they make up 75% of empty slots: the longer the wait, the worse it gets.
5. Maths: empty slots cost about £2m of revenue a year, and the insurers' patients (about £5m a year of first-appointment revenue) are at risk. If empty slots fall back to 100 a week and 25 Saturday sessions see 250 extra patients a week, the list is under the insurers' 3-week line in about 8 to 9 weeks and down to a 2-week wait in 12 weeks.
6. Recommendation: restart reminders with one-tap cancelling, set up a short-notice list and check the waiting list now; run temporary Saturday clinics for about 3 months (each session earns about £2,000 against £1,500 of cost); tell the insurers the plan; track empty slots, the list and the wait every week. As waits fall, fewer patients miss appointments, so the gains build on each other.

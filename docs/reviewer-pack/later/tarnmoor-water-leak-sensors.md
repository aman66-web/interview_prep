# Tarnmoor Water: should it invest in leak sensors?

Case type: operations · Industry: Water utility · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 30 minutes · Style: interviewer_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/tarnmoor-water-leak-sensors

## 1. What the candidate is told

Tarnmoor Water supplies drinking water to about a million people across a largely rural region of northern England. About a quarter of the water it treats leaks out of its pipes before it reaches a tap. Its regulator has told it to cut leakage by 20% within five years, and last summer a dry spell forced a six-week hosepipe ban. The operations director wants to fit acoustic sensors, which listen for leaks, across the whole pipe network over about 18 months, starting with the town centres, where most leaks are repaired. The board approves investments like this only if they pay back within five years. The chief executive has asked your team whether this investment is worth making and, if so, how to make it work. To start: what factors would you consider in deciding whether Tarnmoor should invest in the sensors?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The company.** Tarnmoor Water supplies about 450,000 homes and businesses, roughly a million people, in a largely rural region of northern England. It runs 10,000 km of water mains: old iron mains under the town centres, newer plastic mains in the suburbs, and long, mostly iron mains across farmland. It supplies clean water only; another company handles sewage.

**Units.** Water is measured in megalitres. One megalitre is a million litres. Tarnmoor puts about 320 megalitres a day into its network.

**Objective.** The chief executive wants a yes or no on the programme and a plan to make it work. There are two tests. The regulator's target: cut leakage by 20% from today's level within five years. The board's rule for investments like this: the upfront cost must be paid back by net savings within five years.

**Prices and funding.** Tarnmoor's prices are capped for the next five years under its agreement with the regulator, so it cannot raise bills to pay for the sensors. It can fund the programme from its existing investment budget, but only by delaying other projects, such as replacing some old town-centre mains.

**Missing the target.** Missing the leakage target would bring a financial penalty from the regulator and damage Tarnmoor's reputation. No penalty is included in any of the figures in this case.

**Why leakage matters beyond money.** Last summer Tarnmoor's reservoirs ran low and it had to bring in a six-week hosepipe ban. Every litre that does not leak is a litre it does not need to take from rivers and reservoirs. In Tarnmoor's own customer survey, hosepipe bans and visible leaks are among the things customers complain about most.

**How leaks are found today.** Many town-centre leaks show up as water on the road and are reported by the public. Elsewhere, Tarnmoor relies on 30 leak inspectors, who walk the network at night with listening equipment, covering each area in turn. In rural areas an inspector's round usually finds a leak within about a month; most of the rest of its 100 days is spent waiting for it to be pinpointed, for a crew (bursts go first) and for access to the land.

**Leak inspectors.** The 30 inspectors cost about £1.5 million a year. With sensors, most of their night walking would stop, but an inspector would still need to go out and pinpoint the exact spot of each alert before a crew digs. The director's figures assume all 30 carry on exactly as they do now, with no saving. He has not worked out how many inspectors pinpointing the alerts would need; any who are no longer needed could be retrained to work in repair crews.

**Repair crews.** Tarnmoor has 40 repair crews of its own, and they are fully booked. Bursts that cut off supply or flood a road always go first, so rural repairs wait longest, and many need access to a field arranged with the landowner, which can take weeks. The director's figures assume today's crews can keep up with the sensor alerts.

**Repair capacity needed.** The director's own team estimates that fixing alerts as fast as in the trial, across the whole network, would need about four extra repair crews at about £250,000 a year each. That cost is not in the director's figures.

**The proposal.** Battery-powered acoustic sensors clip onto valves and hydrants about every 500 metres, listen at night for the sound of escaping water and send an alert. Most need no digging to fit. The director proposes fitting them across the whole network over about 18 months, starting with the town centres, because that is where most leaks are repaired and the pipes are oldest. The costs and savings are in his estimates.

**The trial.** Last year Tarnmoor tested the sensors for 12 months in one rural district. The sensors heard most leaks within days, and leakage there fell by about a quarter. But leaks still ran for about three-quarters as long as before (about 75 days instead of 100), because each alert still had to be pinpointed and then wait for a crew and for access to the land. The district's crews were told to put sensor alerts ahead of other planned work, though bursts still went first. The district was too small, and 12 months too short, to show whether bursts fell: the £2 million a year of burst savings in the estimates is the supplier's and the director's expectation. There has been no trial in a town centre or on plastic pipes.

**Plastic pipes.** The sensor supplier says its sensors hear leaks well on iron pipes but much less well on plastic pipes. The suburbs are almost all plastic; the town centres are iron; rural mains are mostly iron.

**Bursts.** Bursts are sudden, large leaks that can cut off supply or flood a road. They cost Tarnmoor about £8 million a year in emergency repairs and compensation to customers, and happen in all three areas; Tarnmoor has not split these costs by area. The supplier and the director expect sensors to catch some leaks before they turn into bursts.

**Recent history.** Tarnmoor's crews now repair about a third more leaks a year than they did five years ago, but total leakage has stayed at about a quarter of the water put into supply.

**Other ways to cut leakage.** Tarnmoor could also replace old mains or lower the water pressure at night. Tarnmoor's engineers estimate that replacing mains would cost far more for each megalitre saved and take many years, and that lowering pressure is cheap but suits only some districts. The director has not costed faster repairs or land-access agreements on their own. The chief executive wants a decision on the sensors first.

**Customers and competition.** Tarnmoor is the only water supplier in its region, so households cannot switch. Fitting the sensors would cause very little disruption; repairs in town centres need roadworks.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Benefits: water no longer treated and pumped, fewer bursts, meeting the regulator's leakage target, fewer hosepipe bans and less water taken from rivers
- Costs: upfront (sensors, fitting, software) and running (data, batteries, licence), plus knock-on costs such as extra repair crews
- Where the water is lost: which parts of the network leak most and why, and whether the sensors work on those pipes
- Will it work in practice: the trial evidence, enough repair crews and inspectors to act on alerts, the supplier and the technology
- The decision tests and alternatives: payback within the board's five years and the sensors' life, the 20% target, and options such as replacing mains or lowering pressure

Good signs:
- Ties the structure to the two tests: the regulator's 20% target and the board's five-year payback rule
- Asks where water is actually lost, not just where leaks are repaired
- Sees that finding a leak saves nothing until it is repaired, so asks about repair capacity
- Asks what evidence supports the savings estimate (the trial) and whether it applies to every part of the network
- Includes public-service factors: hosepipe bans, water taken from rivers, capped bills and disruption to customers
- Asks how long leaks take to be found compared with how long they wait to be repaired, and whether faster repairs and agreed land access would cut leakage alongside the sensors

Red flags:
- Treats it as a pure finance question and ignores the regulator's target and the drought risk
- Assumes sensors fix leaks on their own, with no thought for who repairs them
- Recommends replacing all the old mains without considering cost or time
- Recites a generic profitability framework with no link to leakage

## 4. Exhibits (shown when the candidate asks for the right data)

### Leaks by area of the network, last year (days estimated from night-time flow records)

Shown when the candidate: Ask where the leaks are, where the water is lost, or for leakage broken down by area or type of pipe.

| Area | Mains (km) | Leaks repaired | Average days a leak ran before repair | Water lost to leaks (megalitres a day) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Town centres (old iron mains) | 2000 | 4000 | 10 | 8 |
| Suburbs (newer plastic mains) | 2000 | 800 | 50 | 8 |
| Rural areas (mostly iron mains) | 6000 | 3200 | 100 | 64 |
| Total | 10000 | 8000 | 50 | 80 |

What the data says: Town centres have half of all leaks repaired (4,000 of 8,000) but lose only 10% of the water, because leaks there are spotted and fixed in about 10 days. Rural areas have 40% of the leaks but lose 80% of the water (64 of 80 megalitres a day), because a rural leak runs about 100 days before it is fixed. Less obvious: in every area the water lost is in line with leaks multiplied by days running (40,000, 40,000 and 320,000 leak-days), so a typical leak loses about the same everywhere. The problem is how long leaks run, not how many there are, and rural mains actually have fewer leaks per km than town mains (53 against 200 per 100 km). Sensors cut only the time before a leak is found: in the rural trial a leak's run fell from about 100 to 75 days. That is still worth most in rural areas (about 25 days saved on each of 3,200 leaks, 80,000 leak-days or 16 megalitres a day, against at most 10 days on each town leak), but the larger part of the rural delay is pinpointing, crews and land access. Starting in the town centres, as the director proposes, puts the sensors where they save least water. The suburbs lose as much water as the towns from a fifth of the leaks, because plastic-pipe leaks run about 50 days, and the supplier says its sensors hear plastic pipes poorly. It also fits the fact that five years of extra repairs have not cut leakage: more repairs do little while each rural leak still runs for months.

### Sensors across the whole network: the operations director's estimates

Shown when the candidate: Ask what the programme would cost and save, or for the director's business case.

| Item | Estimate |
| --- | --- |
| Sensors to fit | 20000 |
| Sensors, bought and fitted (£ million, one-off) | 10 |
| Software and set-up (£ million, one-off) | 2 |
| Running costs: data, batteries and software licence (£ million a year) | 1 |
| Leakage cut (megalitres a day) | 20 |
| Treatment and pumping saved per megalitre a day of leakage cut (£ thousand a year) | 100 |
| Emergency burst repairs saved (£ million a year) | 2 |
| Expected life of the sensors (years) | 10 |

What the data says: The upfront cost is £12 million (£10 million of sensors plus £2 million of software). Yearly savings are £4 million: £2 million of treatment and pumping (20 x £100,000) and £2 million of burst repairs. Less £1 million of running costs, that is £3 million a year, so payback is 4 years: inside the board's five-year rule and well inside the sensors' 10-year life, with about £30 million of net savings over that life against the £12 million cost. Half the gross saving comes from fewer bursts, not from the water itself, and the trial did not test bursts: at £1 million a year of burst savings payback would be 6 years, and with none it would be 12, beyond the sensors' life. Payback here counts years of full savings; because savings build up over the 18-month rollout, payback from the first spend, if the £12 million is spent at the start, is nearer 4¾ years. The 20 megalitres a day (25% of leakage) is the rural trial's result applied to the whole network; rural areas alone would give about 16 (a quarter of 64), exactly the 20% target, with no headroom. The figures include no extra repair crews and no change to the 30 inspectors.

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** What percentage of the water lost to leaks comes from rural areas?

Inputs: rural_lost = 64, total_lost = 80
Expected answer: 80 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Rural areas lose 64 of the 80 megalitres a day: 64 / 80 = 0.8, so 80%. That comes from only 40% of the leaks repaired (3,200 of 8,000), while town centres have half the leaks but lose only 10% of the water.

**Question 2.** Using the director's estimates, how many years of net savings would it take to pay back the upfront cost of the whole-network programme?

Inputs: sensors_cost = 10, software_cost = 2, running_cost = 1, leakage_cut = 20, saving_per_mld_k = 100, burst_saving = 2
Expected answer: 4 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Step 1, upfront cost: £10m of sensors plus £2m of software is £12m. Step 2, water saved: 20 megalitres a day x £100,000 a year is £2m a year. Step 3, add £2m of burst repairs saved: £4m a year. Step 4, take off £1m of running costs: £3m a year net. Step 5, payback: £12m / £3m = 4 years, inside the board's five-year rule and well inside the sensors' 10-year life. This counts years of full net savings once the network is running; because savings build up over the 18-month rollout, payback from the first spend, if the £12m is spent at the start, is nearer 4¾ years (accept 4 as the answer).

**Question 3.** If the director's estimate is right, by what percentage would the programme cut Tarnmoor's leakage?

Inputs: leakage_cut = 20, leakage_today = 80
Expected answer: 25 (accepted within 2%)
Working: 20 / 80 = 0.25, a 25% cut. The regulator's 20% target is 16 megalitres a day (20% of 80), so the estimate beats it by 4 megalitres a day.

**Question 4.** The director's team estimates that fixing alerts as fast as in the trial, across the whole network, would need four extra repair crews at about £250,000 a year each, which his figures leave out. Including them, how many years would payback take?

Inputs: sensors_cost = 10, software_cost = 2, running_cost = 1, leakage_cut = 20, saving_per_mld_k = 100, burst_saving = 2, extra_crews = 4, crew_cost_k = 250
Expected answer: 6 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Four crews x £250,000 is £1m a year more. Net savings fall from £3m to £2m a year (£4m of savings less £1m running costs less £1m of crews), so payback is £12m / £2m = 6 years: outside the board's five-year rule, though still inside the sensors' 10-year life.

## 6. Brainstorm

Suppose the board says yes. What would Tarnmoor need to put in place so that the sensors actually cut leakage, and how would it get past the obstacles?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- Change the rollout order: start in rural areas, where 80% of the water is lost, rather than the town centres
- Roll out in phases, with a go or no-go after each one based on leakage measured from night-time flows in each district
- Plan repair capacity before the alerts start, because an alert that waits weeks for a crew saves nothing; agree a contractor for busy periods
- Move the inspectors from night walking to pinpointing sensor alerts, and retrain those no longer needed to join repair crews, so that extra crews cost less
- Rank alerts by likely water loss and burst risk so crews go to the biggest leaks first, and screen out false alarms
- Agree access with farmers and landowners in advance so rural repairs are not held up, and plan roadworks permits in town centres
- Test other kinds of sensor on the plastic suburban pipes before buying, or leave the suburbs out until one works
- Set up a small team or control room to watch alerts and send them straight to the repair job system
- Buy on results: tender the contract, ask for battery-life guarantees and link part of the payment to leaks found
- Explain the programme to customers (fewer hosepipe bans, less water taken from rivers) and make it easy to report leaks
- Use the sensor data to choose which old mains to replace later, and pair sensors with lower night-time pressure where it suits
- Report progress against the 20% target to the regulator each year, so any shortfall is spotted early

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Rural areas lose 80% of the water (64 of 80 megalitres a day) from 40% of the leaks, because leaks there run about 100 days, so the rollout should start rural, not in the town centres
- On the director's figures the programme pays back in 4 years (£12m upfront, £3m a year net) and cuts leakage by 25%, passing both the board's five-year rule and the regulator's 20% target
- The savings depend on repairing leaks quickly: if four extra crews are needed, payback stretches to 6 years, so repair capacity has to be planned before the sensors go in

Good signs:
- Goes beyond a good answer ('yes, it pays back in 4 years') to a better one that says what the 4 years depends on and how to protect it
- Notes that the 25% comes from a rural trial: rural areas alone give about 16 megalitres a day, exactly the target, so town centres and plastic suburbs may add little and there is no headroom
- Points out that half the gross saving (£2m of £4m) rests on the burst estimate, which the trial did not test: on the director's other figures, at £1m a year payback is 6 years, and with no burst saving it is 12 years, beyond the sensors' 10-year life, so the phases should measure bursts as well as leakage
- Works out that, with the four extra crews, payback returns to five years only if about 8 of the 30 inspectors (£0.4m a year) can be freed and moved into repair crews, and asks whether that many can be spared when every alert still needs pinpointing
- Sees that sensors cut only the time before a rural leak is found (about 25 of its 100 days), so faster pinpointing, repairs and land access are where the further gains are

- Proposes phases gated on measured leakage, and weighs public-service benefits (fewer hosepipe bans, less water taken from rivers, capped bills) alongside payback
- Names risks and next steps: sensors on plastic pipes, false alarms, landowner access and the supplier contract

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify the two tests: the regulator's 20% cut within five years (16 of today's 80 megalitres a day) and the board's five-year payback rule. Prices are capped, so the money comes from the existing budget. Confirm the proposal: 20,000 sensors across the whole network, starting in the town centres.
2. Structure: benefits (water, bursts, the target, drought resilience and rivers), costs (upfront, running and knock-on repair and staff costs), where the water is lost and whether sensors work there, whether it will work in practice (trial evidence, repair capacity, supplier), and alternatives such as replacing mains or lowering pressure.
3. Exhibit: rural areas lose 80% of the water from 40% of the leaks, while town centres have half the leaks but only 10% of the water. Water lost follows leaks multiplied by days running, so the problem is how long leaks run. Sensors cut only the time before a leak is found: in the rural trial a leak's run fell from about 100 to 75 days. That is still worth most in rural areas (about 25 days saved on each of 3,200 leaks, 80,000 leak-days or 16 megalitres a day, against at most 10 days on each town leak), but the larger part of the rural delay is pinpointing, crews and land access. The suburbs' plastic pipes are a warning: leaks there run long and the sensors hear plastic poorly.
4. Payback on the director's figures: £12m upfront; £2m of water plus £2m of bursts less £1m running is £3m a year, so 4 years, inside the rule and the 10-year life. Leakage falls 25% against a 20% target.
5. A good answer stops there and says yes. A better one tests the figures: the 25% comes from a rural trial, and rural areas alone give about 16 megalitres a day, exactly the target with no headroom. The savings need fast repairs, and four extra crews (£1m a year) would push payback to 6 years. Payback returns to five years only if about 8 of the 30 inspectors (£0.4m of their £1.5m a year) can be freed and moved into repair crews, which is uncertain because every alert still needs pinpointing. Half the gross saving (£2m of £4m) rests on the burst estimate, which the trial did not test: at £1m a year, payback is 6 years, and with none it is 12, beyond the sensors' life. And savings build up over the 18-month rollout, so payback from the first spend is nearer 4¾ years.
6. Recommendation: approve, in phases. Fit rural areas first, with repair capacity and landowner access agreed before the alerts start; trial other sensors on plastic pipes before covering the suburbs; do the town centres last: they lose only 10% of the water, and leaks there are already fixed in about 10 days. Gate each phase on measured leakage and bursts, and report progress to the regulator. Risks: repair capacity, the untested burst saving, performance on plastic pipes, false alarms and the supplier. Next steps: redo the business case for the rural phase alone, cost faster rural repairs and land-access agreements, plan crews and inspectors, tender the supplier contract and set the rural rollout schedule.

# Orvessa: why have paid subscribers stopped growing?

Case type: growth · Industry: Education technology · Difficulty 3 of 3 · About 35 minutes · Style: candidate_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/orvessa-subscriber-growth

## 1. What the candidate is told

Orvessa is a US language-learning app. Its number of paid subscribers grew quickly for several years, but it has been stuck at about 500,000 for the last year. The CEO says marketing is working as well as ever. She wants to know why growth has stalled and how to get it going again. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The app.** Orvessa teaches 12 languages through short daily lessons on phones. Anyone can use a free version with limits and ads; a paid subscription removes them. Most users are in the US.

**Objective.** The CEO wants 600,000 paid subscribers by the end of next year, about six quarters from now, without raising marketing spend.

**Plans and prices.** There are two paid plans with the same features: annual, at $120 a year paid up front (so $10 a month), and monthly, at $15 a month. Prices have not changed for three years and are in line with competitors.

**Marketing and new subscribers.** Marketing spend rose from $5 million to $5.5 million a quarter over the last two years, and new paid subscribers rose from about 100,000 to 110,000 a quarter. The marketing cost per new paid subscriber has stayed at about $50.

**Free users and conversion.** Orvessa has about 11 million free users, up from 10 million two years ago. About 1% of them become paid subscribers each quarter, and that share has not changed in two years.

**Revenue.** Subscription revenue is about $19.5 million a quarter, up from about $13.8 million in the same quarter two years ago. An annual plan counts as $30 a quarter and a monthly one as $45. The finance team reads the rise as a sign that the business is healthy.

**Checkout.** Just under two years ago the checkout screen was redesigned. It now shows the monthly plan first, already selected; the annual plan is behind a 'See other plans' link. Before the change, about 6 in 10 new subscribers chose the annual plan. Now about 9 in 10 choose monthly.

**Why the checkout was changed.** The product team wanted the first payment to look smaller ($15 rather than $120), expecting more free users to subscribe. The share of free users subscribing stayed at about 1% a quarter, so the change did not lift conversion.

**How subscribers leave.** Monthly subscribers can cancel at any time. Annual subscribers leave by not renewing: about 4 in 10 do not renew, and because renewal dates are spread through the year, that works out at about 10% of annual subscribers leaving each quarter. Leaving rates for each plan have not changed in two years.

**Why people cancel.** In exit surveys the most common reasons are 'not using it enough' and 'finished what I needed it for, such as a trip'. Very few mention price or the quality of the app.

**Early use.** Subscribers who take a lesson on at least 4 days in their first two weeks cancel about half as often as those who do not.

**Product and competition.** App store reviews are mostly positive and have not changed. Competitors have not changed their prices or launched anything major.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Subscriber flow: new subscribers in, cancellations out
- Acquisition: free users, conversion to paid, marketing spend and cost per subscriber
- Retention: who leaves, how often and why, by plan and by group of subscribers
- Plans and value: annual versus monthly, how people choose at checkout, and revenue per subscriber over their life
- What changed around the time growth slowed, the levers, their size, and how to test them

Good signs:
- Splits net growth into new subscribers minus cancellations before looking for causes
- Checks acquisition quickly and moves on once it is clear new subscribers kept rising at a steady cost
- Breaks cancellations down by plan and separates a change in plan mix from a change in each plan's leaving rate
- Asks what changed in the product, checkout or prices around the time growth slowed
- Compares the lifetime revenue of annual and monthly subscribers, not just what each pays per quarter

Red flags:
- Recommends more marketing spend without looking at cancellations
- Assumes the app has got worse without checking each plan's leaving rate
- Lists retention ideas without first finding what changed
- Takes rising quarterly revenue as proof that the checkout change worked, without asking how long each plan's subscribers stay

## 4. Exhibits (shown when the candidate asks for the right data)

### Paid subscriber flow in one quarter (thousand)

Shown when the candidate: Ask how many subscribers join and how many leave each quarter, now compared with before growth stalled.

| Measure | Same quarter two years ago | Last quarter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subscribers at start of quarter | 400 | 500 |
| New paid subscribers | 100 | 110 |
| Cancellations | 64 | 110 |
| Subscribers at end of quarter | 436 | 500 |

What the data says: New subscribers rose 10% (100,000 to 110,000), but cancellations rose by more than 70% (64,000 to 110,000). The share of subscribers leaving each quarter rose from 16% to 22%, so cancellations now equal new subscribers and the base stands still.

### Paid subscribers by plan

Shown when the candidate: Ask how cancellations differ by plan, or how the mix of plans has changed.

| Plan | Price ($) | Billed | Share of subscribers two years ago (%) | Share of subscribers now (%) | Leave each quarter, then and now (%) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Annual | 120 | Once a year | 70 | 40 | 10 |
| Monthly | 15 | Every month | 30 | 60 | 30 |

What the data says: Each plan's leaving rate is unchanged, but the mix has flipped from 70% annual to 60% monthly. Monthly subscribers leave three times as often as annual ones, so the change in mix alone explains the rise from 16% to 22%.

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** What percentage of subscribers left last quarter?

Inputs: cancellations = 110, start = 500
Expected answer: 22 (accepted within 2%)
Working: 110,000 / 500,000 = 22%. In the same quarter two years ago it was 64,000 / 400,000 = 16%.

**Question 2.** Using each plan's leaving rate and today's plan mix, what percentage of subscribers would you expect to leave each quarter?

Inputs: annual_share = 40, annual_leave = 10, monthly_share = 60, monthly_leave = 30
Expected answer: 22 (accepted within 2%)
Working: 40% x 10% = 4 points, and 60% x 30% = 18 points, so 22% in total. That matches the actual 22%, so the change in plan mix explains the whole rise. At the old mix: 70% x 10% + 30% x 30% = 7 + 9 = 16%.

**Question 3.** Roughly how many times more revenue does an average annual subscriber bring in over their life than an average monthly subscriber?

Inputs: annual_price = 120, annual_leave = 10, monthly_price = 15, monthly_leave = 30
Expected answer: 2 (accepted within 15%)
Working: An annual subscriber pays $30 a quarter and 10% leave each quarter, so they stay about 1 / 10% = 10 quarters (2.5 years, the same as 4 in 10 not renewing each year): about $300. A monthly subscriber pays $45 a quarter and 30% leave each quarter, so they stay about 1 / 30% = 3.3 quarters (about 10 months): about $150. $300 / $150 = 2. A monthly subscriber pays 50% more each quarter, which is why revenue rose faster than the number of subscribers, but is worth about half as much over their life.

**Question 4.** If today's 500,000 subscribers had the plan mix of two years ago, and 110,000 new subscribers still joined each quarter, by how many would the base grow each quarter, in thousands?

Inputs: new_subscribers = 110, start = 500, old_annual_share = 70, annual_leave = 10, old_monthly_share = 30, monthly_leave = 30
Expected answer: 30 (accepted within 4%)
Working: At the old mix, 70% x 10% + 30% x 30% = 16% leave each quarter, and 16% of 500,000 = 80,000. With 110,000 joining, the base grows by 110,000 - 80,000 = 30,000 a quarter, so the change in plan mix explains the whole stall. At that pace the base would pass 600,000 in just over a year, a little slower than 30,000 a quarter suggests because cancellations rise as the base grows.

**Question 5.** If nothing changes, so 110,000 people keep joining each quarter and 9 in 10 of them choose the monthly plan, where will the number of paid subscribers eventually settle, in thousands?

Inputs: new_subscribers = 110, new_annual_share = 10, annual_leave = 10, monthly_leave = 30
Expected answer: 440 (accepted within 5%)
Working: Each plan settles where the number leaving equals the number joining. Annual: 11,000 join a quarter (10% of 110,000) and 10% leave, so it settles at 11,000 / 10% = 110,000. Monthly: 99,000 join and 30% leave, so it settles at 99,000 / 30% = 330,000. Total 440,000: if nothing changes, the base will shrink by about 60,000 from today, with three in four subscribers on the monthly plan.

## 6. Brainstorm

What could Orvessa do to keep more of its paid subscribers, or to move more of them onto annual plans?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- Show the annual plan first again, with the saving made clear ($10 a month against $15)
- Offer an annual plan paid in monthly instalments ($10 a month for 12 months), so a small first payment no longer means the monthly plan
- Offer monthly subscribers a discounted switch to annual after their first month or two
- Let people pause their subscription between trips instead of cancelling
- Build a habit in the first two weeks with goals, streaks and reminders
- Spot subscribers who have stopped taking lessons and nudge them back before they cancel
- Make an offer at the moment of cancelling, such as a discount or a cheaper plan
- Win back recent leavers with offers timed to moments such as the new year or before summer travel
- Add a family or two-person plan
- Add a middle option, such as a six-month plan, for people learning for one trip
- Set longer learning goals, such as a level to reach, so subscribers have a reason to stay after a trip

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Growth stalled because cancellations rose from 64,000 to 110,000 a quarter (16% to 22% of subscribers), while new subscribers kept rising at a steady cost
- Each plan's leaving rate is unchanged: the shift from 70% annual to 60% monthly after the checkout redesign explains the whole rise
- Recommendation: make the annual plan the main choice again and move monthly subscribers onto annual plans, rather than spending more on marketing

Good signs:
- Uses lifetime revenue: an annual subscriber is worth about twice a monthly one ($300 against $150), even though a monthly subscriber pays more each quarter, which is why rising revenue hid the problem
- Sizes the prize: at the old mix the base would grow by about 30,000 a quarter, the whole of the stall
- Shows the cost of doing nothing: with 9 in 10 new subscribers choosing monthly, the base will drift down towards about 440,000
- Notes that the mix will take several quarters to shift back, so reaching 600,000 in about six quarters also needs existing monthly subscribers moved onto annual plans and better early habits
- Proposes testing the new checkout on a share of visitors first, and notes that the redesign never raised the share of free users who subscribe, so going back carries little risk

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: paid subscribers stuck at about 500,000 for a year; a goal of 600,000 by the end of next year (about six quarters) without more marketing; two plans with the same features, annual ($120 a year, so $10 a month) and monthly ($15 a month).
2. Structure: net growth = new subscribers minus cancellations. Check acquisition (free users, conversion, cost per subscriber), then retention by plan and why people leave, then what changed around the time growth slowed.
3. Analysis: new subscribers rose from 100,000 to 110,000 a quarter at a steady $50 each, and 1% of free users still subscribe each quarter, but cancellations rose from 64,000 to 110,000 (16% to 22% of subscribers). Acquisition is fine; retention is the problem.
4. Root cause: each plan's leaving rate is unchanged (10% a quarter for annual, 30% for monthly), but the mix moved from 70% annual to 60% monthly after the checkout redesign made monthly the default (about 9 in 10 new subscribers now choose it, against 4 in 10 before). 40% x 10% + 60% x 30% = 22%, which explains the whole rise. The redesign did not raise the share of free users who subscribe.
5. Why it was missed: revenue rose from about $13.8 million to $19.5 million a quarter (about 41%, while subscribers rose 25%), partly because a monthly subscriber pays $45 a quarter against $30. But over their life an annual subscriber brings in about $300 against $150 for a monthly one, so each new subscriber is now worth less.
6. Sizing: at the old mix, 16% would leave each quarter and the base would grow by about 30,000 a quarter, the whole of the stall. If nothing changes, the base drifts down towards about 440,000.
7. Recommendation: test an annual-first checkout with the saving clearly shown (or an annual plan paid monthly), offer existing monthly subscribers a discounted switch to annual, and add a pause option and first-two-weeks habit features. Track the plan mix of new subscribers and the leaving rate each month, and hold marketing spend flat until retention improves. Risks: some people may not subscribe if pushed towards a 12-month commitment, and the mix will take several quarters to shift back, so the six-quarter goal is tight.

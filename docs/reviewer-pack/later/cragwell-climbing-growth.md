# Cragwell Climbing: how to double revenue in five years

Case type: growth · Industry: Leisure · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 30 minutes · Style: candidate_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/cragwell-climbing-growth

## 1. What the candidate is told

Cragwell Climbing runs eight indoor climbing centres in English cities and made £16 million of revenue this year. Its owners want to double revenue to £32 million within five years while staying profitable. They have asked you to work out how. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The company.** Cragwell has 8 indoor climbing centres in English cities. Each has tall walls climbed with ropes and a bouldering area (shorter walls climbed without ropes, above thick mats). It opened them over the last 12 years.

**Revenue.** £16 million this year, about £2 million per centre, and similar across centres. A breakdown of revenue per centre by stream is available.

**Profit today.** Each centre makes an operating profit of about £400,000 a year, about 20% of revenue, before head-office costs and loan interest. Daytime programmes need extra instructors but use walls that are already there, so management expects them to earn at least the same margin.

**Objective and budget.** The owners want revenue of £32 million in year 5, with every centre staying profitable. They will invest up to £20 million in new centres over the five years, from cash and a bank loan.

**Customers and demand.** Members are mostly regular climbers aged 20 to 40; casual visitors, families and groups buy day passes. Assume demand in Cragwell's cities keeps growing by a few per cent a year, and that most new climbers start with bouldering because it needs no ropes or training.

**How busy the centres are.** Centres are full on weekday evenings and at weekends, with queues for walls. Weekday daytimes are quiet. Data on how much of each centre's capacity is used, by time slot, is available.

**Growth at existing centres.** Management estimates that daytime programmes, such as school and university groups, holiday camps, daytime memberships and corporate team events, could lift revenue per existing centre by about 25% within three years. Peak times are already full.

**Prices.** Prices are in line with local rivals. A rise of more than a few per cent would push members to other walls.

**New centres.** Cragwell could open full centres like today's or smaller bouldering-only centres. Figures for both formats are available. Bouldering centres fit ordinary industrial or retail units, which are easy to find; full centres need very tall buildings, which are rare.

**Where new centres would go.** Research has found about 15 towns and city districts in England with enough climbers and no bouldering wall nearby, most of them outside Cragwell's current cities. Assume new centres in those places would not take customers from Cragwell's existing centres.

**Opening pace.** The team can open up to three new centres a year. A new centre's first full year of trading is the year after it opens, so only centres that open by the end of year 4 count towards year 5 revenue.

**Buying rivals.** Some independent climbing walls might be for sale, but none has been identified. Treat buying rivals as an idea, not part of the numbers.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Revenue today: centres x revenue per centre, by stream
- Growth at existing centres: capacity at peak and off-peak, prices, new programmes
- Growth from new centres: format, build cost, revenue, payback and time to reach full revenue
- Constraints: the £20 million budget, three openings a year, sites and the year 5 deadline
- Other options such as buying rivals, and the risks of each route

Good signs:
- Works out the size of the gap (£16 million) and splits it between existing and new centres
- Spots that peak times are full, so growth at existing centres must come from daytimes
- Compares the two formats on payback and fit with the budget, not just on revenue
- Asks where new centres would go and whether they would take customers from existing centres
- Checks the plan against the opening pace and the year 5 deadline

Red flags:
- Proposes a big price rise despite rivals nearby
- Assumes existing centres can simply take more climbers at peak times
- Counts new centres at full revenue without checking time to mature or the budget
- Builds the plan on buying rivals, although none has been identified

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** If daytime programmes lift revenue at each existing centre by 25%, how much revenue will the 8 existing centres make in year 5, in £ million?

Inputs: centres = 8, revenue_per_centre_k = 2000, uplift_pct = 25
Expected answer: 20 (accepted within 3%)
Working: £2 million x 1.25 = £2.5 million per centre. 8 centres x £2.5 million = £20 million, which leaves a £12 million gap to £32 million.

**Question 2.** How many bouldering-only centres would close the rest of the gap to £32 million?

Inputs: target_m = 32, existing_m = 20, bouldering_revenue_k = 1200
Expected answer: 10 (accepted within 3%)
Working: The gap is £32 million - £20 million = £12 million. £12 million / £1.2 million per bouldering-only centre = 10 centres.

**Question 3.** How much would those bouldering-only centres cost to build, in £ million?

Inputs: centres_needed = 10, build_cost_k = 1500
Expected answer: 15 (accepted within 3%)
Working: 10 x £1.5 million = £15 million, within the £20 million budget and leaving about £5 million in reserve.

**Question 4.** If Cragwell closed the same £12 million gap with full centres instead, how much would they cost to build, in £ million?

Inputs: gap_m = 12, full_revenue_k = 2000, full_cost_k = 5000
Expected answer: 30 (accepted within 3%)
Working: £12 million / £2 million per full centre = 6 centres. 6 x £5 million = £30 million, £10 million over the £20 million budget. A full centre reaches mature revenue only in its third full year, so all 6 would also have to open by the end of year 2 (the team's limit of three a year), in tall buildings that are rare.

**Question 5.** How many years does a bouldering-only centre take to pay back its build cost from its operating profit?

Inputs: build_cost_k = 1500, profit_k = 300
Expected answer: 5 (accepted within 3%)
Working: £1.5 million / £300,000 a year = 5 years. A full centre takes £5 million / £400,000 = 12.5 years, and needs until its third full year to reach mature revenue.

## 6. Brainstorm

Besides opening new centres, what could Cragwell do to grow revenue at its existing centres?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- School, college and university sessions in weekday daytimes
- Holiday camps and after-school clubs for children
- Cheaper daytime-only memberships for students, shift workers and older climbers
- Corporate team-building events on weekdays
- Coaching courses, from beginner sessions to rope-climbing qualifications
- Yoga, strength and fitness classes in spare studio space, as part of a premium membership
- A better café with a work-friendly area to bring people in during the day
- Off-peak prices and a booking app to spread climbers away from the busiest hours
- A small peak-time supplement on day passes, of no more than a few per cent so that climbers do not switch to rivals
- Selling or renting more gear, such as shoes and chalk, and offering shoe resoling
- Outdoor climbing trips and guided days for members
- Birthday parties and group bookings on weekday afternoons

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Doubling to £32 million needs both levers: about £4 million from daytime programmes at existing centres and about £12 million from new centres
- About 10 bouldering-only centres close the gap for about £15 million, within the £20 million budget; full centres would cost about £30 million
- Bouldering-only centres pay back in about 5 years against 12.5 for full centres, and make their mature revenue from their first full year

Good signs:
- Turns it into a timeline: two to three openings a year, all open by the end of year 4
- Considers a mix (one full centre and nine bouldering-only centres just fits the budget at £18.5 million) and explains why bouldering-only is still better: faster payback, more cash in reserve and no hunt for a tall building
- Names the risks, such as opening 10 centres in four years when the first 8 took 12, rival bouldering walls opening in the same towns, and the 25% daytime uplift not arriving
- Gives milestones to track, such as daytime capacity used and revenue per new centre

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: £32 million of revenue in year 5 from £16 million today, every centre staying profitable (each makes about 20% today), with up to £20 million to invest and at most three openings a year.
2. Structure: revenue = centres x revenue per centre. Split growth into existing centres (by stream and time slot) and new centres (by format), and check it against the budget and the timeline.
3. Existing centres: memberships and day passes make 70% of revenue, but peaks are 90% to 95% full while weekday daytimes are only 30% used. Daytime programmes could lift each centre by 25%, to £2.5 million, or £20 million across the eight, and they use walls that are already there.
4. Gap: £12 million must come from new centres. Full centres: 6 needed, £30 million (£10 million over budget) and a 12.5-year payback; because they mature only in their third full year, all 6 would have to open by the end of year 2, in tall buildings that are rare. Bouldering-only centres: 10 needed, £15 million, a 5-year payback and mature revenue from their first full year. A mix with one full centre and nine bouldering-only centres just fits (£18.5 million) but leaves little reserve and ties cash up in the slower format.
5. Feasibility: 10 openings by the end of year 4 means two to three a year, within the team's limit of three; suitable units are easy to find and about 15 places have the demand. The biggest risk is execution, since the first 8 centres took 12 years.
6. Recommendation: launch daytime programmes now, open about 10 bouldering-only centres over four years, keep about £5 million in reserve, and review every six months on daytime capacity used and revenue per new centre. Consider buying independent walls if good ones come up.

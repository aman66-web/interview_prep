# Bramblecup: is a UK supermarket deal worth taking?

Case type: market_entry · Industry: Consumer food · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 30 minutes · Style: interviewer_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/bramblecup-uk-supermarket-deal

## 1. What the candidate is told

Bramblecup is a US maker of oat-based, dairy-free yoghurts, sold in grocery stores across the United States. A large UK supermarket chain has offered to stock Bramblecup in all 400 of its stores, on condition that Bramblecup sells only through that chain for its first two years in the UK. Bramblecup's board has asked whether it should accept the offer and launch in the UK. All figures in this case are in pounds. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The company.** Bramblecup makes oat-based yoghurts in several flavours, sold in about 6,000 US grocery stores. It is profitable in the US, where it has built up about 6% of plant-based yoghurt sales over eight years, but it has never sold outside the country.

**Objective.** The board will go ahead only if the UK business breaks even by its second year: that year's sales must cover all of that year's costs. It can fund a loss in the first year, but it cannot afford UK losses after that.

**The offer.** The chain would stock Bramblecup in all 400 of its stores for two years, on its normal terms: its usual margin and standard shelf space, with no help towards launch costs. In return, Bramblecup could not sell anywhere else in the UK during that time, in shops or online. The chain sells about a quarter of all plant-based yoghurt in the UK.

**Why the chain wants exclusivity, and room to negotiate.** The chain wants a new brand that rival supermarkets do not have. It has said it might accept a one-year exclusive instead of two.

**Range and price.** Bramblecup would launch six flavours, all in 400g pots, at about the same shelf price as other branded plant-based yoghurts.

**Production.** Chilled yoghurt keeps for only about four weeks, so it cannot be shipped from the US. A UK contract manufacturer would make it to Bramblecup's recipes.

**Fixed costs.** Running the UK business would cost about £3m a year in each of its first two years: a small team, launch advertising, and in-store tastings and displays. These do not change with sales volume. The team thinks it could launch for about £1.5m a year with much less advertising, but sales would probably build more slowly.

**Market growth.** UK plant-based yoghurt sales grew by about 20% a year until recently, but growth has slowed to about 5% a year.

**Customers.** Shoppers buy plant-based yoghurt mainly to avoid dairy, and many switch brands when one is on promotion. Oat-based yoghurt is a small but growing part of the category; coconut and soya are the largest. In a small UK taste test, shoppers slightly preferred Bramblecup's plain oat yoghurt to the market leader's, but nobody in the test had heard of the brand.

**Competition.** The leading brand has been on UK shelves for more than ten years. The only sizeable oat brand launched about five years ago. Supermarkets also sell their own cheaper own-label ranges.

**Other supermarkets.** Two other large chains, which together sell about 35% of UK plant-based yoghurt, have said they would consider stocking Bramblecup without exclusivity, but only after it has a year of UK sales data. No other large chain would stock it in its first year.

**Other places to sell.** Online grocers and health-food shops would stock Bramblecup straight away, without exclusivity, but together they sell only about 5% of UK plant-based yoghurt.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Market: size and growth of UK plant-based yoghurt
- Competition: who holds share, and how a new oat brand could win
- The deal: what the exclusive listing gives and what it shuts off
- Economics: price, the supermarket's margin, costs per pot, fixed costs and the sales needed to break even
- Risks and alternatives: other ways to enter, or not entering

Good signs:
- Frames the decision around the board's test: break even by the second year
- Works out the sales needed to break even, then asks whether that share is realistic
- Notices that the exclusive deal limits Bramblecup to about a quarter of the market
- Compares the share needed with what existing brands hold, and with Bramblecup's own US share
- Asks what the alternatives to the deal would give, and spots that no other large chain would stock Bramblecup in its first year anyway

Red flags:
- Treats the 400-store offer as good news without asking what it costs Bramblecup
- Talks only about the market and ignores Bramblecup's own economics
- Compares the break-even with the whole UK market (6%) and calls it easy, missing that the exclusive deal limits Bramblecup to the chain's sales
- Assumes success in the US will carry over to the UK without checking

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** How much does Bramblecup keep from each pot after the supermarket's margin and its own costs per pot, in pence?

Inputs: shelf_price = 200, supermarket_margin = 60, making = 55, packaging = 15, delivery = 20
Expected answer: 50 (accepted within 2%)
Working: The £2.00 shelf price minus 60p for the supermarket leaves £1.40. Minus 55p for making, 15p for packaging and 20p for delivery (90p in all) leaves 50p a pot.

**Question 2.** How many pots a year must Bramblecup sell to cover its £3m of fixed costs, in millions of pots?

Inputs: fixed_costs_m = 3, kept_per_pot_p = 50
Expected answer: 6 (accepted within 2%)
Working: £3m is 300 million pence. 300 million / 50p a pot = 6 million pots a year. Across 400 stores that is 15,000 pots a store a year, or about 290 a store a week.

**Question 3.** What yearly sales, measured at shelf prices, does that break-even volume represent, in £ million?

Inputs: break_even_pots_m = 6, shelf_price_p = 200
Expected answer: 12 (accepted within 3%)
Working: 6 million pots x £2.00 = £12m of shelf sales a year, which is 6% of the £200m UK market.

**Question 4.** The chain sells about a quarter of the UK's plant-based yoghurt. Using last year's market size, what percentage of the chain's plant-based yoghurt sales would Bramblecup need to break even?

Inputs: break_even_sales_m = 12, market_m = 200, chain_share_pct = 25
Expected answer: 24 (accepted within 3%)
Working: The chain sells about 25% of £200m = £50m of plant-based yoghurt a year. £12m / £50m = 24%. That is more than the second brand's 20% share of the whole UK market, three times the share of the only sizeable oat brand, and four times Bramblecup's own US share.

**Question 5.** Suppose the chain agrees to a one-year exclusive, and in the best case the two other large chains stock Bramblecup for the whole of its second year. Using last year's market size, what percentage of the three chains' combined plant-based yoghurt sales would Bramblecup need in year 2 to break even?

Inputs: break_even_sales_m = 12, market_m = 200, chain_share_pct = 25, other_chains_pct = 35
Expected answer: 10 (accepted within 3%)
Working: The three chains sell 25% + 35% = 60% of £200m = £120m. £12m / £120m = 10%. That is still above the oat brand's 8% after five years, so a one-year exclusive alone is not enough. With fixed costs of £1.5m, break-even halves to £6m, or 5% of £120m.

## 6. Brainstorm

If Bramblecup still wants to be in the UK, what other ways could it enter, or how could it make this deal work?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- Negotiate a one-year exclusive instead of two, then sell through other chains
- Cut fixed costs with a smaller launch team and less advertising up front, growing spend only as sales come in
- Keep more per pot: a slightly higher price, cheaper chilled delivery through a shared distributor, or a lower margin for the chain in return for exclusivity (an extra 10p a pot, 60p instead of 50p, cuts the break-even volume by a sixth, to 5 million pots)
- License its recipes and brand to a UK yoghurt maker for a royalty, so it carries no fixed costs
- Launch with only the best-selling flavours to keep the range simple
- Sell first through online grocers or health-food shops to build a sales record for the other chains
- Ask the chain to share launch costs, such as free promotional space or in-store tastings, in return for exclusivity
- Partner with or buy a small UK plant-based brand that already has shelf space
- Wait a year and re-check market growth and the other chains' interest
- Use the taste-test result in in-store tastings to win first purchases

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Bramblecup keeps 50p a pot, so it needs 6 million pots, or £12m of shelf sales, a year to cover £3m of fixed costs
- Under the exclusive deal it can reach only the chain's £50m of sales, so it would need 24% of them: more than the second brand's national share, as an unknown brand in a market growing only 5%
- A clear recommendation: do not accept the deal as offered, and name a better route and the conditions for it

Good signs:
- Quantifies the counter-offer: with a one-year exclusive and the two other chains in year 2, break-even needs 10% of their £120m, still above the oat brand's 8%; at £1.5m of fixed costs it needs 5%
- Points out that the other large chains would not stock Bramblecup in its first year anyway, so in year 1 exclusivity only shuts out the small online and health-food channels and its real cost is the second year
- Names risks, such as the other chains still saying no and a smaller launch budget slowing sales
- Names next steps, such as renegotiating with the chain, asking the other chains to put their interest and likely terms in writing, and talking to possible UK licensing partners

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: the offer is a two-year exclusive listing in the chain's 400 stores, with no other UK outlet allowed; the chain sells about a quarter of UK plant-based yoghurt. The board's test is that the UK business breaks even by its second year.
2. Structure: the market (size, growth, competition), Bramblecup's economics (what it keeps per pot, fixed costs, break-even), the deal (what exclusivity gives and what it shuts off), then risks and alternatives.
3. Market: £200m a year at shelf prices, with growth slowing from about 20% to 5% a year. Two brands and own-label hold 80%; the only sizeable oat brand has 8% after about five years, and Bramblecup itself has about 6% in the US after eight.
4. Economics: a £2.00 shelf price, 60p to the supermarket and 90p of costs leaves 50p a pot. £3m of fixed costs needs 6 million pots, or £12m of shelf sales, a year: 6% of the UK market, or about 290 pots a store a week across 400 stores.
5. The deal: exclusivity limits Bramblecup to the chain's £50m of sales, so it would need 24% of them by year 2, more than the second brand's national share. That is very unlikely for an unknown brand in a slowing market. But no other large chain would stock it in year 1 anyway, so in year 1 exclusivity only shuts out online grocers and health-food shops (about 5% of the market); its real cost is year 2.
6. Counter-offer: with a one-year exclusive and the two other chains (35% of the market) stocking it in year 2, break-even needs 10% of the three chains' £120m, still above the oat brand's 8%. Cutting fixed costs to £1.5m brings that to 5%: a stretch, but within reach.
7. Recommendation: decline the deal as offered. Counter with a one-year exclusive and a leaner £1.5m launch budget, and go ahead only if both are agreed and the other two chains confirm in principle that they would consider it after a year of sales data; otherwise license the recipes to a UK maker. Risks: the other chains may still say no, less advertising may slow sales, and shoppers switch brands on promotion. Next steps: negotiate with the chain, ask the other chains to put their interest and likely terms in writing, cost the leaner launch and talk to possible licensing partners.

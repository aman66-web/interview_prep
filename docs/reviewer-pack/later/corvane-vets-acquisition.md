# Corvane Vets: should it buy Kestrel Vale?

Case type: m_and_a · Industry: Veterinary care · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 30 minutes · Style: interviewer_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/corvane-vets-acquisition

## 1. What the candidate is told

Corvane Veterinary Group runs 80 vet clinics for pets across southern England and the Midlands. It has been offered the chance to buy Kestrel Vale Vets, a family-owned chain of 20 clinics in Yorkshire, for £50 million. Corvane's CEO has asked you whether it should buy Kestrel Vale and, if so, how much it should be willing to pay. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The buyer.** Corvane runs 80 clinics treating dogs, cats and other small pets. Its revenue was £120m last year and its operating profit is 20% of revenue, about £24m. It has no clinics in the North of England and has £60m available for deals.

**The target.** Kestrel Vale runs 20 small-pet clinics in Yorkshire. Revenue was £25m last year and has grown about 5% a year for the past three years, mostly from more clients rather than price rises. Its founder is retiring, which is why the family is selling.

**Objective and decision rule.** Corvane wants to grow into the North of England. Its board will only buy a business if last year's operating profit, plus the yearly savings Corvane can realistically make within the first year, is at least 10% of the price paid. Future growth and new services are treated as upside and are not paid for.

**Asking price and deal terms.** The family is asking £50m for the whole company. Kestrel Vale has no debt. The family would consider part of the price being paid later if profit targets are met.

**Buying drugs and supplies.** Kestrel Vale buys drugs and supplies from a local wholesaler. Corvane buys through a national contract and pays about 20% less for the same products, and that contract could cover Kestrel Vale's clinics. The wholesaler contract can be ended with three months' notice.

**Back office.** Kestrel Vale runs its own accounts, payroll, IT and booking system. Corvane could run all of these from its central team within a year.

**Vets and staff.** Kestrel Vale says vets are in short supply in its part of Yorkshire, and it has had vet vacancies open for over a year. It fills about 10% of vet shifts with locum (temporary) vets, who cost about twice as much per day as employed vets. Last year it spent £5.5m on vets: £4.5m on employed vets and £1m on locums. Corvane uses very few locums. Pay for employed vets and nurses is similar at both companies.

**Clients and reputation.** Kestrel Vale has about 90,000 active clients (pet owners). It has a strong local reputation and clients are loyal. Corvane would keep the Kestrel Vale name locally.

**Pet health plans.** Corvane sells a monthly pet health plan (vaccinations, check-ups and discounts) that about 30% of its clients pay for. Kestrel Vale has no plan. There is no data yet on how many Kestrel Vale clients would join, so leave it out of the main numbers.

**Opening its own clinics instead.** Corvane estimates that opening 20 clinics of its own in the North would take about five years, and a new clinic usually loses money for its first two years while it builds up clients.

**Competition rules and integration costs.** Corvane has no clinics near Kestrel Vale's, so assume no competition concerns. One-off integration costs are small enough to ignore in this case.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Strategic fit: does it help Corvane grow into the North, is Kestrel Vale a good business, and is buying better than opening its own clinics?
- Value on its own: Kestrel Vale's current profit and margin
- Savings and extra revenue Corvane could add: drugs, back office, staffing, health plans
- Price versus value against the board's 10% rule
- Risks and integration: keeping vets and clients, how fast savings come

Good signs:
- Asks for the board's decision rule early and uses it to set a maximum price
- Values Kestrel Vale on its own before adding savings
- Compares cost lines with Corvane's to find where savings could come from
- Separates savings that are likely from those that are risky
- Compares buying with opening its own clinics in the North

Red flags:
- Says yes or no to the deal on strategic fit alone, without valuing it
- Counts every possible saving as certain and pays the seller for all of it
- Ignores the risk of losing vets in a vet shortage

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** Under the board's 10% rule, what is Kestrel Vale worth on its own, before any savings, in £ million?

Inputs: profit = 3, required_return_pct = 10
Expected answer: 30 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Price can be at most 10 times yearly profit: £3m / 10% = £30m. That is £20m below the £50m asking price, so the deal only works if Corvane can add value.

**Question 2.** If Kestrel Vale's drugs and supplies fell to Corvane's level as a share of revenue, how much would it save each year, in £ million?

Inputs: revenue = 25, kv_drugs_pct = 20, corvane_drugs_pct = 16
Expected answer: 1 (accepted within 3%)
Working: The gap is 20% - 16% = 4 points of revenue. 4% of £25m is £1m a year. As a check, paying 20% less on £5m of drugs and supplies also saves £1m.

**Question 3.** Counting the drugs saving and moving the back office to Corvane's level, what would Kestrel Vale's yearly operating profit be, in £ million?

Inputs: profit = 3, revenue = 25, kv_drugs_pct = 20, corvane_drugs_pct = 16, kv_back_office_pct = 12, corvane_back_office_pct = 10
Expected answer: 4.5 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Drugs save £1m. Back office is 12% against 10%, so 2% of £25m = £0.5m. Profit becomes £3m + £1m + £0.5m = £4.5m. Both savings can be made within the first year: the wholesaler contract ends on three months' notice and the central team can take over the back office within a year.

**Question 4.** Under the board's rule that yearly profit must be at least 10% of the price, what is the most Corvane should pay, in £ million?

Inputs: profit_with_savings = 4.5, required_return_pct = 10
Expected answer: 45 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Price can be at most 10 times yearly profit: £4.5m / 10% = £45m. Of that, £30m is Kestrel Vale on its own and £15m is the value of Corvane's own savings, so paying the full £45m would hand all of the savings to the seller.

**Question 5.** What yearly operating profit would Kestrel Vale need to justify the £50m asking price under the 10% rule, in £ million?

Inputs: asking_price = 50, required_return_pct = 10
Expected answer: 5 (accepted within 2%)
Working: 10% of £50m is £5m. That is £0.5m more than the £4.5m from drugs and back office. The only other gap is staff (2 points, £0.5m), which is the extra cost of locums: covering every locum shift with an employed vet would turn £1m of locum cost into about £0.5m. So the asking price only works if Kestrel Vale replaces all its locums, which is unlikely within a year in a vet shortage.

## 6. Brainstorm

What are the main risks in this deal, and how could Corvane protect itself or add value after buying?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- Vets may leave after the sale: offer retention bonuses and keep local clinical leaders in charge
- Ask the retiring founder to stay on for a handover period to keep clients and staff
- Check how much of the business depends on the founder personally, such as clients who see only them
- Keep the Kestrel Vale name and prices for the first year so loyal clients do not drift away
- Check the wholesaler contract terms so the drugs saving can start on time
- Move clinics to Corvane's booking system one at a time to avoid disruption
- Check the accounts, leases and equipment carefully before signing
- Pay part of the price later, only if profit targets are met
- Cut locum costs by recruiting vets, for example with a graduate scheme or relocation support
- Launch Corvane's pet health plan with Kestrel Vale's 90,000 clients as extra upside
- Use Kestrel Vale as a base to buy or open more clinics in the North

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Kestrel Vale is worth about £45m to Corvane under the 10% rule, after realistic savings of £1.5m a year (drugs £1m, back office £0.5m)
- The £50m asking price needs £5m of profit, which only works if staff costs also fall, and replacing every locum is unlikely in a vet shortage
- A clear recommendation on price: buy, but pay no more than £45m upfront; offer below that and walk away above it, or pay any extra later only if profit targets are met

Good signs:
- Notes that Kestrel Vale is worth only £30m on its own, so Corvane should not hand all its savings to the seller
- Explains why buying beats building: opening 20 clinics would take about five years and lose money at first
- Names the risk of losing vets and how to manage it
- Treats the pet health plan and future growth as upside, not as part of the price

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: Corvane wants to grow into the North; the board's rule is that last year's profit plus savings achievable in the first year must be at least 10% of the price; the asking price is £50m, there is no debt, and Corvane has £60m available.
2. Structure: strategic fit (including buying versus opening its own clinics), Kestrel Vale's value on its own, savings and extra revenue Corvane could add, price versus value, risks and integration.
3. Analysis: Kestrel Vale makes £3m on £25m (12% margin against Corvane's 20%), so on its own it is worth £30m. The 8-point gap is drugs (4 points), back office (2) and staff (2, the extra cost of locums).
4. Maths: drugs save £1m and back office £0.5m, both within a year, taking profit to £4.5m, which supports a price of up to £45m. The £50m price needs £5m, which also requires the £0.5m staff saving.
5. Judgement: the staff saving means replacing every locum, with vacancies already open for over a year in a vet shortage, so it is the least certain and Corvane should not pay for it upfront. Buying still beats building, which would take about five years.
6. Recommendation: buy, because it is a good fit for growing in the North, but open at around £40m and walk away above £45m upfront. If the family insists on £50m, pay the last £5m later only if profit targets are met. Next steps: check the accounts, leases and wholesaler contract, agree retention plans for vets and a handover with the founder, and treat the health plan as upside.

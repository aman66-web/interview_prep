# Cobalt Basin: why did profit fall in a record snow season?

Case type: profitability · Industry: Travel and leisure · Difficulty 3 of 3 · About 35 minutes · Style: candidate_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/cobalt-basin-ski-resort

## 1. What the candidate is told

Cobalt Basin is an independent ski resort in the US Rocky Mountains. This winter it had record snowfall and more skier visits than ever, yet its profit halved, from $20 million to $10 million. The CEO wants to know why, and what to do before next season. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The resort.** Cobalt Basin owns its lifts, ski school, rental shops and restaurants. It does not own any hotels; accommodation nearby is run by other companies. It is about two hours' drive from a large city.

**Objective.** The CEO wants profit back to at least $20 million next season, without cutting safety or the quality of the slopes.

**Season and weather.** This season's snowfall was the highest on record and conditions were excellent all season. Last season's snowfall was about average. Both seasons were the usual length.

**Prices.** Day-ticket prices did not change, at about $100 per visit on average. Last spring the resort cut the season pass price from $800 to $400 to build loyalty and sell more passes before the season. Data on visits and passes is available.

**Why sell season passes.** Pass money arrives before the season starts and does not depend on the snow, which helps the resort through poor winters. That is why the CEO wanted to sell more of them.

**Visitors.** Day-ticket visitors mostly come from the city or from other states, and many rent equipment or take lessons. Pass holders are mostly locals and city regulars who own their gear. A pass holder skis about 10 days a season.

**Who bought the extra passes.** About three-quarters of this season's extra pass holders had bought day tickets at Cobalt Basin the season before, on average about 5 days each. The rest were new to the resort.

**Spending on extras.** Day-ticket visitors spend about $50 per visit on food, rentals and lessons. Pass holders spend about $20 per visit.

**Costs.** Staff costs are mostly fixed for the season. Snow operations (grooming, clearing roads and car parks, and avalanche control) rose with the record snow. Other costs did not change. A profit and loss by season is available.

**Crowding.** On peak weekends and holidays the lift queues were long and the car parks were full by 9am. Some day-ticket visitors were turned away or went to other resorts. Midweek, the slopes were quiet.

**Competitors.** Two larger resorts nearby kept their season passes at about $900 and their day tickets at about $110.

**Next winter's snow.** Nobody can forecast it. Assume a normal snow year for planning.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Lift revenue: visits by type (day ticket or season pass) x lift revenue per visit
- Revenue from extras: food, rentals and lessons per visit, by type of visitor
- Costs: staff, snow operations and other costs, this season versus last
- What changed: weather, prices, the pass and the visitor mix, crowding
- Fixes for next season and their effect on profit

Good signs:
- Notices the puzzle: visits rose but profit fell, so revenue per visit or costs must explain it
- Separates one-off weather effects from lasting effects of the resort's own decisions
- Segments visitors into day-ticket buyers and pass holders, and asks who bought the extra passes
- Quantifies each driver's share of the fall

Red flags:
- Blames the weather without checking revenue
- Looks only at costs
- Suggests cutting staff or slope quality without evidence
- Recommends scrapping season passes without weighing the cash they bring in before the season

## 4. Exhibits (shown when the candidate asks for the right data)

### Cobalt Basin profit and loss by season ($ million)

Shown when the candidate: Ask for revenue and costs by line, this season versus last.

| Line | Last season | This season |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lift tickets and passes | 48 | 42 |
| Food, rentals and lessons | 22 | 21 |
| Total revenue | 70 | 63 |
| Staff | 25 | 25 |
| Snow operations | 10 | 13 |
| Other operating costs | 15 | 15 |
| Profit | 20 | 10 |

What the data says: Profit fell $10 million. Revenue fell $7 million (lift tickets and passes down $6 million, extras down $1 million) even though visits rose, and snow operations rose $3 million. Lower revenue explains 70% of the fall; the snow costs, which should return to about $10 million in a normal winter, only 30%.

### Visits and season passes by season

Shown when the candidate: Ask how visits, ticket types or pass sales changed, or why lift revenue fell when visits rose.

| Measure | Last season | This season |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day-ticket visits (thousand) | 400 | 300 |
| Average day-ticket price ($) | 100 | 100 |
| Season passes sold (thousand) | 10 | 30 |
| Season pass price ($) | 800 | 400 |
| Visits per pass holder | 10 | 10 |
| Total visits (thousand) | 500 | 600 |

What the data says: Total visits rose 20%, but all the growth was pass visits (from 100,000 to 300,000) while day-ticket visits fell by 100,000. Halving the pass price meant it paid for itself after 4 days instead of 8, so many day-ticket buyers switched (about 15,000 of them, worth about 75,000 day visits) and crowded peak days pushed others away. A pass visit now brings $40 of lift revenue against $100 for a day ticket (it was $80 last season), and lift revenue per visit fell from $96 to $70.

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** What percentage of the $10 million fall in profit comes from lower revenue?

Inputs: lift_last = 48, lift_this = 42, extras_last = 22, extras_this = 21, profit_last = 20, profit_this = 10
Expected answer: 70 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Lift revenue fell $6 million and extras $1 million: $7 million of the $10 million fall, or 70%. The other 30% is the $3 million rise in snow operations, which should not repeat in a normal winter.

**Question 2.** What was lift revenue per visit this season, in dollars?

Inputs: lift_m = 42, visits_k = 600
Expected answer: 70 (accepted within 3%)
Working: $42 million / 600,000 visits = $70 per visit, down from $96 last season ($48 million / 500,000 visits).

**Question 3.** Counting both the pass and spending on extras, how much did the resort make from each pass-holder visit this season, in dollars?

Inputs: pass_price = 400, visits_per_pass = 10, pass_extras = 20
Expected answer: 60 (accepted within 3%)
Working: $400 pass / 10 visits = $40 of lift revenue per visit, plus $20 of extras = $60. A day-ticket visit brings $100 + $50 = $150, so a pass visit brings in about $90 less than a day-ticket visit. Last season, at $800, a pass visit brought $80 + $20 = $100.

**Question 4.** If pass sales and day-ticket visits had stayed at this season's levels (30,000 passes and 300,000 day visits), what season pass price would have kept lift revenue at last season's $48 million, in dollars?

Inputs: lift_last_m = 48, day_visits_k = 300, day_price = 100, passes_k = 30
Expected answer: 600 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Day tickets brought in 300,000 x $100 = $30 million. The gap to $48 million is $18 million. $18 million / 30,000 passes = $600 per pass.

**Question 5.** Next season, suppose the pass is $600, visits match this season, lift revenue is back at $48 million, extras stay at this season's level and snow costs are back to normal. What profit would the resort make, in $ million?

Inputs: lift_m = 48, extras_m = 21, staff_m = 25, snow_m = 10, other_m = 15
Expected answer: 19 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Revenue: $48 million of lift revenue + $21 million of extras = $69 million. Costs: $25 million staff + $10 million snow operations (last season's normal-snow level) + $15 million other = $50 million. Profit = $19 million, about $1 million short of the $20 million goal; about 7,000 more day visits at $150 each would close it.

## 6. Brainstorm

How could Cobalt Basin bring profit back to at least $20 million next season?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- Raise the season pass price to at least about $600, still well below rivals' $900, with an early-bird discount to keep the cash coming in before the season
- Give this season's pass holders a renewal price so the rise feels fair
- Offer tiers: a cheaper midweek-only pass, when the slopes are quiet, and a full pass that costs more
- Add blackout dates on peak weekends and holidays to the cheaper passes
- Cap the number of season passes sold
- Use dynamic day-ticket prices: higher on peak days, cheaper midweek and when bought in advance
- Sell multi-day ticket packs for visitors who ski a few days a season, so they have a reason not to switch to a pass
- Introduce reservations or paid parking on peak days to manage crowding
- Sell extras to pass holders, such as lesson packs, prepaid food cards and lockers
- Win back day visitors with bus packages from the city and midweek group and school deals
- Add summer activities such as mountain biking and hiking so the lifts earn money all year
- Plan snow operations better, for example with fuel contracts and grooming schedules, to soften costs in heavy snow years

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Profit halved mainly because of the pass price cut, not the snow: lower lift and extras revenue explain $7 million (70%) of the $10 million fall, while one-off snow costs explain $3 million (30%)
- The cheap pass moved day-ticket buyers onto passes and crowded others out on peak days: a pass visit now brings about $60 against $150 for a day-ticket visit, and lift revenue per visit fell from $96 to $70
- Reprice the pass rather than scrap it: about $600 would have kept lift revenue at $48 million at this season's volumes, with tiers or blackout dates to protect peak days for day-ticket buyers

Good signs:
- Checks the target: with lift revenue back at $48 million, extras at about $21 million and normal snow costs, profit is about $19 million, so a pass priced above $600, winning back day visitors or selling more extras must close the last $1 million
- Treats $600 as a floor, not a magic number: it assumes all 30,000 pass holders come back, but at a higher price some will go back to day tickets or leave, and anything up to the old $800 is defensible with rivals at $900
- Keeps the pass's real benefit: cash before the season that does not depend on the snow
- Names the risks: pass holders may react badly to a 50% increase, and rivals may respond
- Proposes what to track, such as spring pass sales and day-ticket visits on peak days

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: profit fell from $20 million to $10 million in a record snow season with more visits. The goal is at least $20 million next season, with no cuts to safety or slope quality. Day-ticket prices did not change, but the pass price was cut from $800 to $400.
2. Structure: profit = revenue - costs. Revenue = visits by type (day ticket or pass) x (lift revenue + extras per visit). Costs split into staff, snow operations and other. Then separate the weather (one-off) from the resort's own decisions (lasting).
3. Diagnosis: revenue fell $7 million (lift tickets and passes $6 million, extras $1 million) and snow operations rose $3 million, so lower revenue is 70% of the fall. Snow costs should return to about $10 million in a normal winter.
4. Root cause: passes tripled to 30,000 at half the price, so a pass paid for itself after 4 days instead of 8. Day-ticket visits fell from 400,000 to 300,000: most of the extra pass holders used to buy day tickets, and crowding on peak days pushed others away. A pass visit now brings about $60 ($40 of lift revenue plus $20 of extras) against $150 for a day-ticket visit, and lift revenue per visit fell from $96 to $70.
5. Maths: at this season's volumes, a pass price of about $600 would have kept lift revenue at $48 million. With normal snow costs, profit would then be about $19 million, so the pass alone falls about $1 million short of the goal.
6. Recommendation: keep the pass, which brings in cash before the season whatever the snow, but reprice it to at least about $600 (still well below rivals' $900), add a cheaper midweek-only pass and blackout dates on peak days, use dynamic day-ticket prices, and sell extras to pass holders to close the last $1 million. Risks: pass holders' reaction to the rise and rivals' response. Track spring pass sales and day-ticket visits on peak days.

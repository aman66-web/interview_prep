# Calder Instruments: should it hedge its dollar income?

Case type: other · Industry: Laboratory instruments manufacturing · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 30 minutes · Style: interviewer_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/calder-instruments-currency-risk

## 1. What the candidate is told

You are a graduate analyst in the global markets team of a UK bank, working with the desk that helps companies manage currency risk. Calder Instruments, a UK maker of laboratory instruments and a long-standing client, sells mostly to the US and is paid in US dollars, but pays most of its costs in pounds. Its finance director is worried that a weaker dollar next year would hurt profit. She has asked us whether Calder should hedge next year's dollar income and, if so, how much of it. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The client.** Calder Instruments is a privately owned UK company that designs and makes benchtop analysers: instruments that measure what a sample is made of, used in university, hospital and drug-company laboratories. It has one factory near Cambridge and about 300 staff. It has banked with us for many years and has a term loan from us.

**Where Calder sells and how it is paid.** About 80% of Calder's sales are to US customers, who are invoiced in US dollars. The rest go to UK and other customers, all invoiced in pounds. US customers pay about 60 days after delivery, and the dollars arrive fairly evenly through the year. Calder's next financial year starts in three months.

**Costs.** Almost all of Calder's costs are in pounds: the factory, staff and most materials. Two costs are in dollars: some electronic components bought from US suppliers, and a small US sales and service office. Calder pays these out of the dollars it receives, so they offset part of its dollar income. Next year's budget is available.

**How Calder handles dollars today.** Calder has never hedged. Each month it pays its dollar costs from the dollars it has received and converts the rest into pounds at that day's market rate (the spot rate).

**The exchange rate in this case.** To keep the maths simple, this case gives the exchange rate as pounds per dollar. In this case, today $1 is worth £0.80, which is the same as £1 buying $1.25. Markets usually quote this pair the second way, as dollars per pound. Calder's budget for next year uses £0.80 per dollar. When the dollar weakens, each dollar Calder receives turns into fewer pounds.

**Which way the dollar will go.** Nobody can reliably say, and our desk does not advise clients on where the rate will go. For planning, our risk team tests a move of 10% either way over a year. In this case 'the dollar weakens by 10%' means each dollar is worth 10% fewer pounds: £0.72 instead of £0.80. 'The dollar strengthens by 10%' means £0.88. The rates are in an exhibit.

**Calder's prices.** Calder publishes its US price list in dollars once a year, and next year's prices are already set. Its main competitors, based in the US and Germany, also price in dollars in the US, so raising dollar prices to make up for a weaker dollar would probably lose orders.

**The loan and its covenant.** Calder pays £1 million of interest a year on its term loan from us, and that amount is fixed. The loan has a covenant: a promise in the loan agreement that the bank checks from the yearly accounts. This one is interest cover, meaning operating profit divided by interest, which must be at least 4 times. So Calder needs operating profit of at least £4 million next year. If a covenant is broken, the bank can renegotiate the loan or ask for it to be repaid early. For this case, dollars sold under a forward count in operating profit at the forward rate, and an option's premium counts as a cost, so the covenant test sees the hedged rate.

**What the finance director wants.** She wants to be sure Calder keeps its covenant next year, and she wants a steadier profit. She does not want to bet on the exchange rate. She also worries about looking foolish if Calder hedges everything and the dollar then strengthens.

**What a forward contract is.** A forward contract is an agreement made today to swap a set amount of one currency for another on a set future date, at a rate fixed today. Calder would agree to sell us a set number of dollars each month next year, and we would pay a fixed number of pounds for each dollar, whatever the market rate is by then. Nothing is paid up front, and the bank's margin is already built into the rate.

**The bank's forward rate.** For forwards covering next year, the bank quotes £0.78 per dollar (about $1.28 per pound). In practice each month would have a slightly different rate; for this case use £0.78 for all of next year. The forward rate is not a forecast. It is today's rate adjusted for the difference between dollar and pound interest rates, with the bank's small margin built in. In this case dollar interest rates are the higher of the two, so the forward gives Calder fewer pounds per dollar than today's rate.

**What a forward commits Calder to.** Calder must deliver the dollars on each date, even if the market rate is better by then. If the dollar strengthens, Calder still gets only £0.78 per hedged dollar and misses the gain. If US customers pay less than expected, Calder still has to deliver the dollars it promised, buying any shortfall at the market rate on the day, which costs it money if the dollar has strengthened.

**If dollars arrive late.** If a customer pays late, Calder can ask us to move a forward's date to when the dollars arrive, with a small adjustment to the rate. It can also use forwards that can be drawn on at any point over a window of dates. Either way it avoids having to buy dollars in the market to deliver on time.

**The bank's limit for Calder's forwards.** Because Calder could end up owing us money under a forward if the dollar strengthens, our credit team has to approve a limit for its forwards. It has approved forwards of up to $40 million for next year.

**How sure next year's US sales are.** Calder's sales team has split next year's $50 million of US sales by how certain each part is. The breakdown is available.

**If US sales fall short.** Lower sales would cut profit by more than the costs Calder saves, so they are a separate threat to the covenant. The finance director's budget is her central case, and our lending team is discussing covenant headroom against lower sales with her separately. For this question, take the budgeted sales as given when testing the covenant; the hedge protects against the exchange rate, not against lower sales.

**Options.** The bank could also sell Calder an option: the right, but not the duty, to sell dollars to us at £0.75 in each month next year. It costs an up-front premium of £0.01 per dollar covered (£10,000 per $1 million). If a dollar is worth more than £0.75 on the day, Calder simply converts at the market rate instead, so it keeps any gain from a stronger dollar, and if the dollars never arrive it has nothing to deliver. Because Calder pays the premium up front and never has to deliver, it can never end up owing us money under an option, so options use none of the $40 million limit for forwards.

**Changing costs or sales in the longer term.** Calder could buy more components from US suppliers, move some assembly or servicing to the US, or grow sales outside the US. None of this could be in place before next year.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- The exposure: how much income and cost is in dollars, and the net amount Calder has to turn into pounds
- The impact: operating profit in pounds at today's rate and if the dollar moves 10% either way, against the £4 million covenant
- How certain the dollar income is: firm and recurring sales against expected and new ones
- Hedging tools and what they cost: a forward (fixed rate, nothing up front, gives up the upside, must deliver) and an option (premium, keeps the upside, no duty to deliver)
- The recommendation: how much to hedge, with which product, spread over which months, and within the bank's limit

Good signs:
- Nets the dollar costs off the dollar sales before sizing the exposure (a natural hedge)
- Turns the 10% move into pounds of profit and tests it against the covenant, instead of talking about currencies in general
- Looks at both directions: what happens if the dollar strengthens, with and without a hedge
- Explains that the forward rate is fixed today and is not a forecast, so the gap to today's rate comes from interest rates (and the bank's margin), not from a view on the dollar; the pounds given up buy certainty
- Sizes the hedge to how sure the dollar income is, and warns against promising more dollars than Calder will receive
- Remembers the bank's side: the $40 million credit limit for forwards, and that the bank is both Calder's lender and the seller of the hedge, so the hedge must be sized to Calder's real needs

Red flags:
- Uses the $50 million of gross dollar sales and forgets the $5 million of dollar costs
- Bases the answer on a guess about where the dollar will go
- Recommends hedging all forecast sales, or more, without asking how certain they are
- Treats the forward as free, or as a way to make money, and ignores the gain given up if the dollar strengthens
- Recommends the largest hedge the $40 million limit allows, or argues for hedging mainly because it protects the bank's loan

What the data says: Half the US sales ($25 million) are signed or recurring. Hedging the full $45 million, or the $50 million of gross sales, means promising dollars that may never arrive.

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** At today's rate of £0.80 per dollar, what operating profit does Calder's budget give for next year, in £ million?

Inputs: usd_sales = 50, usd_components = 2, usd_office = 3, gbp_sales = 10, gbp_costs = 40, rate_today = 0.8
Expected answer: 6 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Net dollar income is $50m - $2m - $3m = $45m, worth $45m x £0.80 = £36m. Add £10m of pound sales and take off £40m of pound costs: £36m + £10m - £40m = £6m. (Line by line: sales £40m + £10m = £50m, costs £40m + $5m x 0.80 = £44m, profit £6m.) That is 6 times the £1m of interest.

**Question 2.** If Calder hedges nothing and the dollar weakens by 10%, so each dollar is worth £0.72 instead of £0.80, by how much does next year's operating profit fall, in £ million?

Inputs: usd_sales = 50, usd_components = 2, usd_office = 3, rate_today = 0.8, rate_weak = 0.72
Expected answer: 3.6 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Only the net $45m ($50m - $2m - $3m) is turned into pounds. Each dollar is worth £0.08 less (0.80 - 0.72), so profit falls by $45m x £0.08 = £3.6m: from £6m to £2.4m. A 10% currency move takes 60% off profit, and interest cover falls to 2.4 times, breaking the 4 times covenant. Using the $50m of gross sales gives £4.0m, which forgets that the dollar costs fall too.

**Question 3.** If Calder sold all of its net dollar income for next year forward at the bank's rate of £0.78, how many pounds would it give up compared with converting at today's rate, in £ million?

Inputs: usd_sales = 50, usd_components = 2, usd_office = 3, rate_today = 0.8, rate_forward = 0.78
Expected answer: 0.9 (accepted within 3%)
Working: The forward gives £0.02 less per dollar than today's rate (0.80 - 0.78). On the net $45m that is $45m x £0.02 = £0.9m. In return, profit is fixed at £5.1m ($45m x £0.78 = £35.1m, + £10m - £40m) whatever the dollar does: £0.9m below the budget, but well above the £4m covenant, against a possible £3.6m fall if Calder does nothing. Hedging all $45m is more than the bank's $40m limit would allow, so this is a benchmark only. The £0.9m comes mostly from the interest-rate gap, plus the bank's small margin; it is not a separate fee and not a forecast of the dollar.

**Question 4.** What is the smallest amount of dollars Calder must sell forward at £0.78 so that, if the dollar weakens by 10% to £0.72, operating profit is still at least 4 times its £1 million of interest? Answer in $ million.

Inputs: covenant_multiple = 4, interest = 1, usd_sales = 50, usd_components = 2, usd_office = 3, gbp_sales = 10, gbp_costs = 40, rate_weak = 0.72, rate_forward = 0.78
Expected answer: 26.6667 (accepted within 3%)
Working: The covenant needs operating profit of 4 x £1m = £4m. Unhedged, a 10% weaker dollar leaves $45m x £0.72 + £10m - £40m = £2.4m, so Calder is £1.6m short. Each dollar sold forward brings £0.78 instead of £0.72, £0.06 more. £1.6m / £0.06 = about $26.7m, so roughly $27m. Hedging about $30m leaves a little room: profit of £4.2m in the weak case ($35m gives £4.5m).

## 6. Brainstorm

Beyond a single forward contract with the bank, what else could Calder do to protect its profit from a weaker dollar, now and over the next few years?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- Use an option for the less certain income: the right to sell dollars at £0.75 for £0.01 a dollar keeps the gain if the dollar strengthens, and there is nothing to deliver if the sales never come
- Hedge in layers: forwards for the firm and recurring income now, then add more as new orders are signed
- Spread the forwards across the months the dollars arrive, so each one matches a customer payment
- Agree a written hedging policy with the board (for example, hedge a set share of the next 12 months' net dollar income on a rolling basis), so decisions do not depend on guesses about the rate
- Buy more components from US suppliers, so more costs offset the dollar income (a bigger natural hedge)
- Move some assembly, final testing or servicing to the US
- Add a currency clause to large US contracts, or raise US prices when the list is next reset (risk: competitors also price in dollars)
- Invoice more customers outside the US in pounds or euros, and grow UK and European sales
- Borrow part of its debt in dollars, so some interest and repayments are paid from dollar income (though dollar interest rates are higher in this case)
- Ask the bank for forwards that can be used over a window of dates, or moved, so a late customer payment or a slipped delivery does not force Calder to buy dollars to deliver
- Talk to the bank early about covenant headroom if a weak dollar looks likely to push interest cover close to 4 times

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Calder's net dollar exposure is $45 million ($50 million of sales less $5 million of dollar costs); a 10% weaker dollar cuts operating profit by £3.6 million, from £6 million to £2.4 million, which breaks the £4 million covenant (4 times £1 million of interest)
- The forward fixes £0.78 per dollar, £0.02 below today's rate: hedging all $45 million gives up £0.9 million against today's rate but fixes profit at £5.1 million whatever the dollar does
- A clear recommendation to hedge part, not all: roughly $27 million to $35 million (for example about $30 million). At least about $27 million keeps the covenant if the dollar weakens 10%; no more than about $35 million, the net dollar income Calder is likely to receive; all within the bank's $40 million limit

Good signs:
- Shows the range with about $30 million hedged: profit of about £4.2 million to £6.6 million if the dollar moves 10% either way (£4.5 million to £6.1 million with $35 million), against £2.4 million to £9.6 million unhedged
- Explains why not 100%: only $25 million of US sales is signed or recurring, and with a forward Calder must deliver the dollars even if sales fall short, perhaps buying them at a worse rate
- Says the forward rate is not a forecast: hedging buys certainty and gives up the gain if the dollar strengthens, which answers the finance director's worry about looking foolish
- Suggests a mix: forwards for the firm part and an option or later forwards for the uncertain part, spread across the months the dollars arrive
- Recognises the bank's two roles (lender, and seller of the hedge with a margin in the rate): the hedge must be sized to Calder's real exposure and explained fairly, including the gain it gives up and the duty to deliver, and Calder makes the decision. It should not be pushed because it protects the bank's own loan
- Points out that a hedge protects against the rate, not against lower sales: missing the $10 million of new-customer orders would take £8 million off revenue, which could hurt profit more than a 10% weaker dollar
- Notes the budget's £0.80 cannot be locked in: the best Calder can fix is £0.78, a profit of £5.1 million, which is why a company might set its budget rate at the forward rate
- Sees that an option does not do the covenant work: after the £0.01 premium a £0.75 option nets £0.74 a dollar, only £0.02 more than doing nothing if the dollar weakens 10% (options alone on all $45 million would leave profit at £3.3 million), so forwards cover the firm part and an option suits the uncertain part and bigger falls

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: Calder earns about 80% of its sales in dollars but pays most costs in pounds. The finance director wants to keep the covenant (operating profit at least 4 times £1 million of interest, so £4 million) and a steadier profit, without betting on the rate. Next year's US prices are set, and no operational change can happen in time.
2. Structure: size the exposure (dollar income less dollar costs), measure the effect of a 10% move either way on profit and the covenant, check how certain the dollar income is, compare the hedging tools and their cost, then recommend how much to hedge and how.
3. Exposure and impact: net dollar income is $45 million ($50 million - $2 million of components - $3 million for the US office). At £0.80 profit is £6 million ($45 million x 0.80 + £10 million - £40 million). If the dollar weakens 10% to £0.72, profit falls £3.6 million to £2.4 million, interest cover 2.4 times, so the covenant breaks; if it strengthens to £0.88, profit rises to £9.6 million. A 10% currency move swings profit by 60%.
4. Cost of hedging: the forward fixes £0.78, £0.02 below today's rate. Hedging all $45 million gives up £0.9 million against today's rate but fixes profit at £5.1 million; that is more than the bank's $40 million limit allows, so it is a benchmark only. The £0.02 comes mostly from the gap between dollar and pound interest rates, plus the bank's small margin. It is what Calder gives up against its budget rate in return for certainty, not a forecast: the budget's £0.80 cannot be locked in.
5. How much: only $25 million of US sales is signed or recurring (about $20 million net of dollar costs), and about $35 million net is likely if repeat customers buy as usual. Hedging $45 million or more risks promising dollars that never arrive. To keep the covenant in the weak case Calder needs at least about $27 million hedged ((£4 million - £2.4 million) / £0.06).
6. Recommendation: sell about $30 million forward at £0.78 now (two-thirds of the net exposure; anything from about $27 million to $35 million is defensible, all within the bank's $40 million limit), spread across the months the dollars arrive, adding forwards up to about $35 million as repeat orders are signed. Profit then ranges from about £4.2 million (dollar 10% weaker) to £6.6 million (10% stronger), instead of £2.4 million to £9.6 million, and the covenant holds in the budget case. This is about $10 million more than the near-certain $20 million, because hedging only $20 million would leave profit of £3.6 million if the dollar weakens 10%. If repeat orders fall short and the dollar strengthens to £0.88, buying $10 million to deliver would cost about £1 million ($10 million x (£0.88 - £0.78)); that is the risk Calder accepts. The hedge does not protect against lower sales, which could threaten the covenant more and are for our lending team to discuss with the finance director. Consider an option at £0.75 for part of the uncertain income and agree a written hedging policy. Because the bank is both lender and seller of the hedge, explain the trade-offs fairly and let Calder decide. Longer term, build natural hedges: more costs in dollars and more sales in pounds or euros.

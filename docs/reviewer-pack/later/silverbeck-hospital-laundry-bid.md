# Silverbeck Linen: should it bid for a hospital contract?

Case type: growth · Industry: Commercial laundry · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 30 minutes · Style: interviewer_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/silverbeck-hospital-laundry-bid

## 1. What the candidate is told

Silverbeck Linen is a family-owned commercial laundry with one plant in the north of England. It washes sheets, towels, gowns and theatre scrubs for private hospitals, care homes and hotels. Its chief executive wants to grow the business, but without putting it at risk. A hospital trust that runs four hospitals near the plant is closing its own laundry and has opened a tender: one supplier will wash all of the trust's linen for five years, and the trust will choose between the bids mainly on price. Two national laundry groups are expected to bid. The chief executive has asked you: should Silverbeck bid, and if it does, what should its bid offer? How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**The company.** Silverbeck has one plant and about 200 customers: private hospitals, care homes and hotels. It washes about 28 million pieces a year. Most customers hire their linen from Silverbeck, which buys, owns and replaces it and charges one price per piece washed.

**Revenue and profit today.** Revenue is about £14 million a year: 28 million pieces at an average of 50p each. Costs are £12.6 million, so operating profit is £1.4 million, a 10% margin. All figures exclude VAT.

**Objective and the chief executive's tests.** The chief executive has two tests for any new contract. First, the contract's own profit, counted before the one-off set-up cost, must be at least 10% of its revenue, the margin the business makes today, and she means in every year of the contract, not only the first. Second, the set-up cost must be paid back from that profit within three years; this is the only test the set-up cost is judged by. She also does not want service to existing customers to suffer, and she wants room left for private customers to keep growing.

**The tender.** The trust's own laundry closes in 12 months because its building is old and would cost too much to replace, so the contract starts then and runs for five years, after which the trust will tender again. The winner washes, dries, irons or folds and delivers all the linen for the four hospitals, every day including weekends. One supplier wins all four hospitals. The trust scores bids 60% on price and 40% on quality, which covers delivery reliability, the hygiene standard and the bidder's staffing plan.

**Volume and price.** The trust expects about 14 million pieces a year, half of Silverbeck's volume today, and will pay for at least 12 million a year even if it sends fewer. Its guide price is 40p a piece, and its draft contract holds that price for all five years. Price is scored on the average price per piece across the five years, so a bid may set a different price for each year, or a different price below a set volume, as long as every price is written into the bid.

**Why the price is below Silverbeck's average.** The trust owns its linen, so this is wash-only: Silverbeck would not buy or replace any linen for it, which saves about 4p a piece compared with its hire customers. The rest of the gap is size: Silverbeck's other customers are far smaller and pay small-customer prices, while the trust expects a price for a very large contract.

**How the contract's costs are built.** The operations director has built the contract's costs from the plant's own operating figures rather than from today's accounts (share the cost build-up exhibit). There are two kinds. Some come with every piece washed: energy and water by the kilo, and the bags and ward labels each piece is packed with. Others come in blocks that are needed from the first day, whatever the volume: a night shift and a Sunday shift, an engineer and a supervisor on site while they run, and the daily van routes. Rent, rates, managers and the office are already paid for and are not counted against the contract.

**Costs over the five years.** The finance director has projected the contract's costs for each year if the price stays at 40p (share the projection exhibit). It is built from Silverbeck's own records: pay for night and weekend work has risen about 8% a year over the past four years, faster than its other costs, because several large warehouses in the town compete for the same night workers; energy and chemicals have risen about 4% a year. Existing customers have their prices reviewed every year, so these rises have been passed on to them. The trust's draft contract would not allow that.

**Costs if only the guaranteed 12 million pieces come.** The costs that come with every piece fall with the volume. The blocks do not: both new shifts, the engineer and supervisor cover and all six van routes are still needed to serve four hospitals every day.

**Set-up cost and funding.** About £1.5 million, paid once before the contract starts (share the contract exhibit for the breakdown). Silverbeck can pay it from its own cash, but it would use about half of its cash reserve.

**Plant capacity.** The plant's two shifts, Monday to Saturday, can wash about 32 million pieces a year. It washes 28 million today, and private volume has been growing by about 1 million pieces a year. The new night shift could wash up to 12 million pieces a year and the Sunday shift up to 4 million. The operations director would run the trust's linen only on the new shifts, to keep its hygiene checks and delivery times separate and to leave the day shifts' spare room for private customers.

**Staffing.** The new shifts need 80 staff: 60 for the night shift and 20 for Sundays, paid at night or weekend rates. Silverbeck already has 8 unfilled jobs on its current shifts. Agency staff cost it about a third more per hour than its own employees. None of the trust's laundry staff will move across: the trust is moving them all to other jobs within the trust before its laundry closes, although some may prefer laundry work and could apply to Silverbeck.

**Energy.** Silverbeck buys gas and electricity on one-year fixed-price deals, so its energy price can change every year. Its supplier has said it would quote for longer deals if asked.

**Delivery and penalties.** Silverbeck delivers 99% of orders on time today. The furthest of the four hospitals is about 40 miles from the plant. If fewer than 98% of a month's deliveries are on time, the trust deducts 5% from that month's invoice.

**The rival bidders.** Two national laundry groups are expected to bid. Both are much larger and already run night shifts at their own plants, so they may need fewer new staff and could bid low. Their nearest plants are 70 to 90 miles from the hospitals. The trust has said that reliability counts, but price carries the most weight.

**Customers today.** Silverbeck's largest customer is a private hospital group at about 10% of revenue. No other customer is above 5%.

**Hygiene standard.** The trust requires a hospital infection-control standard for washing its linen. Silverbeck already meets that standard for its private hospital customers.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- The decision rule: the chief executive's two tests (a 10% margin in every year, set-up paid back within three years), no harm to existing customers and room left for private growth
- Winning the tender: how the trust scores bids (60% on the five-year average price, 40% on quality), what the two national groups can offer, and how Silverbeck should price its bid
- The contract's own economics: what each piece leaves after the costs that come with it, the blocks of cost for the new shifts and van routes, and the one-off set-up cost
- How those economics change over five years: a price held at 40p while pay and energy rise, and what happens at the guaranteed minimum volume
- Ability to deliver: shift capacity, hiring 80 night and weekend staff, delivery reliability and the hygiene standard

Good signs:
- Asks how the trust will choose between bids and what the rival bidders are likely to offer, not only whether the contract would pay
- Separates the one-off set-up cost from the yearly costs
- Builds the contract's own costs from how the plant works (per kilo, per shift, per van route) rather than from Silverbeck's total costs today
- Asks whether the price can change during the five years, and how fast Silverbeck's own pay and energy costs have been rising
- Asks whether the 10% test has to be met in the first year only or in every year
- Asks who would staff the night and Sunday shifts, and how much room the contract would leave for private customers

Red flags:
- Judges the contract on its first year alone, as if costs would stay where they are for five years
- Treats the new staff as a cost per piece, as if a night shift could be hired a piece at a time
- Leans towards yes because revenue would grow by 40%, before looking at costs
- Treats the guaranteed 12 million pieces as a safety net for all five years without checking it against rising costs
- Mixes the one-off set-up cost into the yearly costs
- Rejects 40p straight away because it is below today's average cost of 45p a piece, which includes rent, managers and linen the contract does not add
- Discusses hospital laundry in general rather than this tender and this plant

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** From the operations director's build-up, how much of the 40p price is left from each piece in year one, after the costs that come with every piece, in pence?

Inputs: price_pence = 40, energy_per_kg = 16, water_per_kg = 6, kg_per_piece = 0.5, bags_per_piece = 1
Expected answer: 28 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Energy and water are priced by the kilo: (16p + 6p) x 0.5 kilo = 11p a piece. Add 1p for bags, ward labels and tracking: 12p a piece. 40p - 12p = 28p left from every piece to pay for the new shifts, the cover and the van routes. The new staff are not in this figure because they come in whole shifts, not by the piece.

**Question 2.** In year one, how many pieces a year must the contract handle for that 28p a piece to cover the new shifts, the cover and the van routes, in millions?

Inputs: night_k = 1800, sunday_k = 300, cover_k = 240, routes = 6, route_k = 100, contribution_pence = 28
Expected answer: 10.5 (accepted within 3%)
Working: The blocks: night shift £1,800k + Sunday shift £300k + cover £240k + 6 routes x £100k = £2,940k a year, the same at any volume the two new shifts can wash. £2,940k / 28p = 10.5 million pieces. The trust pays for at least 12 million, so in year one even the guaranteed volume covers the blocks, with 1.5 million pieces to spare.

**Question 3.** At the trust's estimate of 14 million pieces, what profit would the contract make in year one, before the set-up cost, in £ million?

Inputs: pieces_m = 14, breakeven_m = 10.5, contribution_pence = 28
Expected answer: 0.98 (accepted within 3%)
Working: Every piece above the 10.5 million break-even adds 28p: 3.5 million x 28p = £0.98m. Revenue is 14 million x 40p = £5.6m, so the margin is 17.5%, well above the 10% test (£0.56m). At this rate the £1.5m set-up cost is paid back in under two years. In year one the contract looks clearly worth having; what matters now is whether it stays that way at a price held for five years.

**Question 4.** Using the finance director's projection, how much would the contract lose in year five at the trust's estimate of 14 million pieces, in £ million?

Inputs: blocks_y5_k = 3780, pieces_m = 14, price_pence = 40, piece_cost_y5 = 14
Expected answer: 0.14 (accepted within 5%)
Working: By year five each piece's own costs are 14p, leaving 26p, so 14 million pieces bring in 14 million x 26p = £3.64m towards blocks that now cost £3.78m: a loss of about £0.14m. The profit falls by £280k every year (0.5p more per piece is £70k at 14 million, plus £210k more on the blocks): £0.98m, £0.70m, £0.42m, £0.14m, then minus £0.14m. So at a fixed 40p the contract fails the 10% test from year three and loses money in year five; at the guaranteed 12 million pieces it starts losing money in year three.

## 6. Brainstorm

The contract is worth having in its first years and not in its last. What could Silverbeck put in its bid, or do before the start, to keep it worth having for all five years, and what else could go wrong?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- A volume price step: a higher price per piece in any year the trust sends well below its estimate (for example, under 13 million pieces), because the shifts, cover and routes cost the same at 12 million as at 14 million
- Keep room for private customers: run the trust's linen only on the night and Sunday shifts, cap the contract at what those shifts can wash, and plan for the day the day shifts fill up, since private customers pay more per piece than the trust
- A staffing plan that slows the pay drift: hire the 80 staff directly well before the start, agree a night and weekend rate for two or three years, train day staff to cover Sundays, and approach the trust's laundry staff who would rather keep working in a laundry, so agency hours stay low
- Shape the bid's price for the tender's scoring: because price is scored on the five-year average, start below 40p and rise each year (for example 38p rising by 2p a year, an average of 42p), so the later years are protected without the high flat price a rival could undercut
- Win on the 40% for quality: a plant no more than 40 miles from any of the hospitals, against the national groups' 70 to 90 miles, a 99% on-time record, the hygiene standard already met, and a staffing plan the trust can check
- Make the blocks cheaper: fewer, fuller van routes, or routes shared with private hospital customers near the four hospitals, and one engineer covering both new shifts
- Energy: ask the supplier to quote a fixed price for several years for the contract's share of gas and electricity
- As a fallback, ask for the price to be reopened after year three if pay or energy has risen faster than an agreed rate
- Missing the 98% on-time target and paying penalties (about £23k for each bad month): dedicated vans for each hospital, a spare van, and a start that brings the hospitals on one at a time
- Service to existing customers slipping: keep the trust's work on its own shifts and lines, and track existing customers' on-time rate every week
- Depending on one customer for nearly 30% of revenue: keep winning private customers, and plan early for the trust's next tender at the end of year five
- Hygiene failures or disputes over lost linen: regular audits against the trust's standard, and agree at the start how linen is tracked and who pays for losses
- Creative extra: later offer the trust linen hire to replace its own ageing linen, at a higher price per piece, once the service has proved itself

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- An answer first: bid, but not at a flat 40p for five years. Offer a price that rises each year (or, failing that, one that can be reopened after year three), a higher price if volume falls well below the estimate, and a staffing plan for the new shifts; or a clear 'not on the trust's draft terms'
- Year one: 28p left from each piece after its own costs, £2.94m of blocks, break-even at 10.5 million pieces, so about £0.98m of profit at 14 million (17.5%, well above the 10% test) and the £1.5m set-up cost paid back in under two years
- The five-year path: at a fixed 40p the profit falls by about £280k a year, fails the 10% test from year three and turns into a loss of about £0.14m in year five; at the guaranteed 12 million pieces it loses money from year three

Good signs:
- Follows the fixed price through all five years instead of stopping at year one: about £0.98m, £0.70m, £0.42m, £0.14m, then a loss of £0.14m, so £1.68m of the five years' £2.1m comes in the first two years and only about £0.6m is left after the set-up cost
- Sees that the guarantee protects less each year: the volume needed to cover the blocks rises from 10.5 million pieces in year one to about 12.4 million in year three, above the guaranteed 12 million, and about 14.5 million in year five, above the trust's estimate. At the guaranteed volume the contract fails the 10% test even in year one (£0.42m on £4.8m, under 9%), loses about £0.6m over the five years before the set-up cost and never pays the set-up cost back
- Uses the tender's scoring: price is scored on the five-year average, so a rising price protects the later years at a lower scored price than a flat one. 38p rising by 2p a year (an average of 42p) keeps about 5p a piece every year, margins of about 11% to 13% and about £0.7m a year (about £2m over the five years after the set-up cost); a flat price that still passed the test in year five would need to be about 46p. Sees too that no shape works at an average of 40p: the contract's costs average about 37p a piece over the five years, so about 41p is the floor for a 10% margin overall
- Weighs the rivals: both national groups already run night shifts, so they may need fewer new staff and bid low, but their plants are 70 to 90 miles away, so their routes cost more and reliability is harder to promise. Silverbeck's best case is on the 40% for quality, not on matching the lowest price
- Sees the staffing plan as the biggest cost lever: pay is about £2.34m of the £2.94m blocks and rises fastest. If a quarter of the new shifts' hours came from agencies at a third more per hour, the blocks would rise by about £175k (£2.1m x 25% x one third) and the contract would fail the test from year two instead of year three
- Weighs capacity as a cost: the contract fills 14 of the new shifts' 16 million pieces. Private volume (about 29 million when the contract starts, growing about 1 million a year) fills the two day shifts by about the end of year three and then needs the 2 million of night-shift room that is left, which runs out at about the end of the contract. Private customers pay about 50p (about 46p without the linen), so in the later years the contract takes room that private work would fill at a better price
- Notes that 40p is below today's average cost of 45p a piece, but the contract only has to cover the costs it adds (33p a piece in year one), because rent, rates, managers and the office are already paid for, and that this gap closes as the contract's costs climb to about 41p a piece by year five
- Gives risks with a way to reduce each, such as the volume price step, the staffing plan and dedicated vans, and clear next steps
- Speaks to the chief executive in 30 to 60 seconds, answer first

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: the chief executive's two tests (the contract's profit, before the set-up cost, at least 10% of its revenue in every year; the set-up cost paid back within three years), no harm to existing customers and room for private growth. The tender: one supplier for five years, scored 60% on the five-year average price and 40% on quality, against two national groups. The contract: about 14 million pieces a year (at least 12 million paid for), a guide price of 40p held for five years, wash-only because the trust owns its linen, daily deliveries to four hospitals. Today: £14m revenue, £1.4m profit, a 10% margin.
2. Structure: winning the tender (the scoring and the rivals); the contract's own economics (what each piece leaves, the blocks of cost, the set-up cost); how those change over five years at a fixed price and at the guaranteed volume; whether Silverbeck can deliver (shifts, staff, delivery, hygiene); then the bid's terms and the risks.
3. Year one: each piece costs 12p in energy, water and bags (0.5 kilo x 22p + 1p), leaving 28p. The night shift, Sunday shift, cover and six van routes are £2.94m of blocks, so break-even is 10.5 million pieces. At 14 million the contract makes 3.5 million x 28p = £0.98m, a 17.5% margin, well above the 10% test, and pays the £1.5m set-up cost back in under two years. At the guaranteed 12 million it makes about £0.42m, under 9%.
4. Five years: Silverbeck's own records show night and weekend pay rising about 8% a year, so the projection adds 0.5p a piece and £210k of blocks each year while the price stays at 40p. Profit falls by £280k a year: £0.98m, £0.70m, £0.42m, £0.14m, then a loss of £0.14m in year five. The contract fails the 10% test from year three; the volume needed to break even passes the guaranteed 12 million in year three and the trust's 14 million in year five. At the guaranteed volume it loses money from year three and never pays back the set-up cost. £1.68m of the five years' £2.1m comes in the first two years.
5. Delivering and winning: the new shifts can wash 16 million pieces, so the trust's 14 million fits, but private growth fills the day shifts by about year three and then uses up the last 2 million of night-shift room by about the end of the contract. 80 new staff are needed in a town where warehouses compete for night workers, and pay is the fastest-rising cost. The rivals already run night shifts and may bid low, but their plants are 70 to 90 miles away; Silverbeck's furthest hospital is 40 miles away and it delivers 99% on time.
6. Recommendation to the chief executive: bid, but not at a flat 40p for five years. Offer a price that rises each year, for example 38p rising by 2p a year: it is scored as 42p, keeps about 5p a piece every year, passes the 10% test in all five years and makes about £2m over the contract after the set-up cost. Add a higher price in any year the trust sends well under 14 million pieces, and back the bid with a staffing plan that hires directly and agrees night and weekend pay for two or three years. Compete on the 40% for quality: closeness, the on-time record and the hygiene standard. If the trust will only take a flat price, the floor is about 46p; if it insists on 40p for all five years, do not bid. Next steps: test the local market for night workers, get multi-year energy quotes, and ask the trust how it would score a rising price and a volume price step.
7. There is no single right answer: 'do not bid' can score as well if it uses the five-year path and the guaranteed volume, and so can a flat bid of about 46p if the candidate weighs its lower chance of winning against the national groups. Weak answers judge the contract on year one alone.

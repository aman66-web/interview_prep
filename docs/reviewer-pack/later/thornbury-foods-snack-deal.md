# Thornbury Foods: what to pay for a snack brand?

Case type: m_and_a · Industry: Food manufacturing · Difficulty 2 of 3 · About 30 minutes · Style: interviewer_led

Status: draft (draft until you sign it off). Page on the preview site: /cases/thornbury-foods-snack-deal

## 1. What the candidate is told

You are a graduate analyst in the mergers and acquisitions team of a UK bank. Thornbury Foods is a UK food manufacturer that makes biscuits, crackers and cakes, mostly as supermarkets' own-label products. Its board wants to buy Hedgewick, a fast-growing brand of baked lentil crisps and oat bars. Hedgewick's owners are selling it through an auction run by their adviser, and they are hoping for about £150 million. Thornbury has hired our bank to advise it on the deal. The board wants to know what Hedgewick is worth, the most Thornbury should pay, and what could go wrong. How would you approach this?

## 2. Facts the interviewer gives only when asked

**Thornbury Foods.** Thornbury has about £700 million of yearly revenue and EBITDA of about £70 million. About 80% of its sales are own-label biscuits, crackers and cakes made for UK supermarkets (sold under the supermarket's own name); the rest are two biscuit brands of its own. It has three factories in England and supplies all the major UK supermarket chains. It is privately owned by its founding family, so it is not listed on a stock exchange.

**Why Thornbury wants the deal.** Thornbury's board believes its own-label margins will keep being squeezed by the supermarkets, and that branded healthy snacks will grow faster and earn better margins than biscuits. It wants a fast-growing brand it can build on. It would rather buy one than build one, because its own new brands have struggled in the past.

**Hedgewick.** Hedgewick was founded seven years ago by two founders. It sells baked lentil and chickpea crisps (about 70% of revenue) and oat bars (about 30%). It owns no factories: the crisps are made by one contract manufacturer (a co-packer, which makes products to someone else's recipe for a fee per pack) and the bars by another. About 80% of sales are to UK supermarkets, 15% to coffee shops and convenience stores and 5% online. Its figures for the last three years are available.

**The figures.** 'This year' in Hedgewick's figures means the financial year that has just ended, so all three years are full, final years. Thornbury's accountants have had a first look and found no one-off items, so the figures can be used as they are. Revenue is net of any discounts given to supermarkets.

**What EBITDA means here.** EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: roughly the profit the business makes from its day-to-day operations, before the cost of its debt and tax. Buyers use it to compare businesses of different sizes and with different amounts of debt.

**How the bank values Hedgewick.** For this first-round offer, our deal team values healthy-snack brands like Hedgewick at 10 times their EBITDA for the latest full year. A multiple is simply the value of the whole business divided by its EBITDA. Use 10 times this year's EBITDA.

**Debt, cash and the price.** Hedgewick has no borrowings, and the sellers will take out any spare cash before the sale. So the price Thornbury pays equals the value of the whole business (its enterprise value); there is no debt to subtract or cash to add. Assume Hedgewick is handed over with a normal level of working capital (stock, money owed by customers and money owed to suppliers), so there is no adjustment for that either.

**Synergies and how the bank values them.** Synergies are savings or extra sales that only happen because the two businesses are combined. Thornbury's operations team has estimated the cost synergies and our bank has checked them, with the yearly saving once each is fully running and the one-off cost of getting there. The estimates are available. For this offer the bank uses a simple rule: value the yearly saving at the same 10 times as EBITDA, then subtract the one-off costs. Ignore tax and the time it takes to reach the full saving.

**When the synergies arrive.** The savings build up over three years and are fully running from the third year after the deal. Most of the one-off costs are paid in the first year.

**How much of the synergies Thornbury will pay for.** Thornbury's board will pay the sellers at most half of the net value of the synergies. Its reasoning: only Thornbury can create the savings, and Thornbury carries the risk if they are not delivered, so it wants to keep at least half of their value.

**Customers.** Hedgewick's largest customer is one national supermarket chain, which took 40% of its revenue this year. The second largest, another supermarket chain, takes about 15%. Hedgewick has no long-term contract with either: it supplies them under rolling agreements with no fixed volumes. The largest customer reviews its snack range once a year and can cut or increase a brand's shelf space; its next review is in about six months. Thornbury already makes own-label biscuits for that chain and knows its buyers well.

**Where the growth came from.** Most of the growth came from more shelf space and new flavours at the largest customer. Sales to all other customers grew much more slowly, by about 13% a year, while sales to the largest customer doubled in two years. The other supermarket chains grew slowest; most of the rest came from coffee shops and online.

**Costs if sales change.** Cost of goods (ingredients, packaging and the co-packers' fees) moves in line with sales, because the co-packers are paid per pack. Marketing and overheads would stay the same in the short term if sales fell, although Hedgewick could cut marketing over time. Delivery is a fixed yearly contract with a haulier.

**Making the crisps at Thornbury.** Thornbury's Midlands factory runs at about 70% of capacity and has spare space and baking ovens, but it has never made crisp-style snacks. It would need a new seasoning and packing line and a separate sealed room, because Hedgewick's crisps are sold as gluten-free and the factory bakes wheat biscuits. The £7 million one-off cost of the move includes this work and a £1 million fee to end the crisp co-packer's contract early. Thornbury has never run a gluten-free line.

**Founders and staff.** Both founders want to sell all their shares. The founder who runs the brand and product development has offered to stay for a year. The founder who runs sales, and who personally manages the relationship with the largest customer, wants to leave when the deal completes. Hedgewick has about 40 staff, mostly in sales, marketing and product development.

**Funding and leverage.** Thornbury would pay in cash, funded by a new loan from its lenders. Its net debt (borrowings minus cash) is £40 million today. Its lenders measure leverage as net debt divided by EBITDA: roughly how many years of EBITDA it would take to repay the debt if all of EBITDA went to repaying it (in practice tax, interest and investment come first). They require Thornbury to stay at or below 3 times. After a deal they would add Hedgewick's EBITDA to Thornbury's, but would not count any synergies until they have been delivered.

**Revenue synergies.** Thornbury believes it could get Hedgewick into more supermarkets and more stores, because it already supplies every major UK chain. Nobody has put a number on this, so the bank treats it as possible upside and does not include it in the price.

**The sale process and other bidders.** The sellers' adviser is running an auction: several possible buyers are invited to bid. First-round offers are due in three weeks and are not binding. The best bidders then get about six weeks of due diligence (detailed checks of the accounts, contracts and customers) before making final, binding offers. The adviser says at least one other company is interested, but we do not know who it is or what it might pay.

**Asking price.** The sellers are hoping for about £150 million. Their adviser argues that Hedgewick's fast growth deserves a higher multiple than usual. The adviser's forecast shows EBITDA of £12.5 million next year, so £150 million is 12 times that forecast. Our bank uses the latest full year instead, because the forecast relies on more growth at the largest customer.

**Talking to Hedgewick's customers.** Every bidder has signed the sellers' adviser's confidentiality letter, which bars it from contacting Hedgewick's customers, co-packers or staff about the sale. In the second round, the adviser may arrange a call with the largest customer's buyers for the best bidders.

## 3. What a good structure includes

Key buckets:
- Hedgewick on its own: growth, margins, where the growth came from, and its stand-alone value on an EBITDA multiple
- Synergies: what they are, how big, the one-off cost of each, how likely each is and when it arrives
- Price: stand-alone value plus a share of the synergies, compared with the £150 million the sellers hope for, and a walk-away price
- Risks: reliance on one supermarket, integration (moving crisp production, gluten-free, founders leaving) and the brand
- The deal itself: funding and leverage, other bidders, the auction timetable and protections such as an earn-out

Good signs:
- Asks how the bank values the business (which multiple, applied to which year's EBITDA) before doing any maths
- Keeps Hedgewick's stand-alone value separate from the value of the synergies
- Asks how certain each synergy is and what it costs to achieve, instead of taking the total as given
- Asks where the growth came from and spots the reliance on one supermarket
- Thinks about how much of the synergy value to give the sellers, and sets a walk-away price
- Checks whether Thornbury can fund the deal and whether the founders will stay

Red flags:
- Recommends paying £150 million because Hedgewick is growing fast, without valuing it
- Values the synergies without subtracting their one-off costs, or treats them all as certain
- Ignores that 40% of Hedgewick's revenue comes from one customer with no long-term contract
- Applies the multiple to revenue instead of EBITDA

## 5. The maths, with working

**Question 1.** Using the bank's multiple of 10 times this year's EBITDA, what is Hedgewick worth on its own, in £ million?

Inputs: ebitda_this = 10, multiple = 10
Expected answer: 100 (accepted within 2%)
Working: This year's EBITDA is £10 million. 10 x £10 million = £100 million. The sellers' hoped-for £150 million is 15 times EBITDA, £50 million above the stand-alone value.

**Question 2.** Using the bank's rule (value the yearly saving at 10 times, then subtract the one-off costs), what are the cost synergies worth, in £ million?

Inputs: buying_saving = 1, delivery_saving = 1, back_office_saving = 0.5, production_saving = 2.5, buying_cost = 0.5, delivery_cost = 0.5, back_office_cost = 2, production_cost = 7, multiple = 10
Expected answer: 40 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Yearly savings add up to £5 million (1 + 1 + 0.5 + 2.5); at 10 times that is £50 million. One-off costs add up to £10 million (0.5 + 0.5 + 2 + 7). £50 million - £10 million = £40 million. Of that, the crisp production move is £18 million (£25 million - £7 million) and the three high-confidence savings £22 million (£25 million - £3 million).

**Question 3.** Thornbury's board will pay the sellers at most half of the synergy value. What is the most Thornbury should pay for Hedgewick, in £ million?

Inputs: standalone_value = 100, synergy_value = 40, share_paid = 0.5
Expected answer: 120 (accepted within 2%)
Working: Stand-alone value £100 million plus half of the £40 million synergy value (£20 million) = £120 million. That is £30 million below the sellers' £150 million. Even handing over every pound of the synergies would only reach £140 million, so £150 million would pay the sellers for more than the whole deal is worth to Thornbury on the savings we can count.

**Question 4.** Suppose the largest customer halved its orders at its next range review. Cost of goods would fall in line with sales, but marketing and overheads would not. What would Hedgewick then be worth on its own, at 10 times EBITDA, in £ million?

Inputs: ebitda_this = 10, largest_customer = 24, cut_share = 0.5, gross_profit = 30, revenue = 60, multiple = 10
Expected answer: 40 (accepted within 5%)
Working: Lost sales: half of £24 million = £12 million. Hedgewick keeps 50p of gross profit on every £1 of sales (£30 million / £60 million), so it loses £6 million of gross profit. Marketing and overheads do not change, so EBITDA falls from £10 million to £4 million, and the stand-alone value from £100 million to £40 million. A 20% fall in revenue would take 60% off the value. This is the first-year hit on the bank's simple rule. Over time Hedgewick could cut marketing, so the lasting fall would be smaller: with marketing back at 20% of the new £48 million of sales (£9.6 million), EBITDA would be about £6.4 million, about £64 million at 10 times. But a brand that has just lost shelf space might also be valued at a lower multiple.

## 6. Brainstorm

What are the biggest risks in this deal, and how could Thornbury protect itself in its offer and the sale agreement?

Ideas a strong candidate might raise:
- The largest customer could cut Hedgewick's shelf space at its range review in about six months; halving its orders would cut EBITDA to about £4 million in the first year and, on the bank's simple rule, the stand-alone value to about £40 million
- If volumes fall, the production and buying savings shrink too, so the downside hits both the stand-alone value and the synergies
- Pay part of the price later (an earn-out), only if sales to the largest customer hold up over the next one to two years; size it against the risk: £20 million paid later covers only a third of the £60 million fall in value if that customer halves its orders
- An earn-out has drawbacks: the sales founder leaves at completion, so the sellers cannot influence the target and will resist it, and it can make the bid look weaker than an all-cash rival's
- Use due diligence to test the relationship with the largest customer: ask the sellers' adviser for a call with the chain's buyers in the second round, and use Thornbury's own knowledge of the chain to judge the answers, not to approach the chain about the deal directly
- The sales founder who manages the largest customer wants to leave: ask them to stay for a handover period, with part of their money paid later
- Thornbury has never made crisps: the taste or texture could change and loyal customers could notice
- Keeping the crisps gluten-free in a wheat biscuit factory needs a sealed room; a mistake could mean product recalls and lasting damage to the brand
- Delay the production move: keep the co-packer at first and move only after a successful trial, or leave it out of the price altogether
- A challenger brand owned by a big own-label manufacturer may lose some of its appeal; keep a separate brand team and identity
- A small, fast-moving team may not fit a large manufacturer's culture, and key staff in sales and product development may leave
- The savings take three years to arrive, while most one-off costs come in year one
- In an auction it is easy to overpay to beat another bidder: decide the walk-away price before bidding and stick to it
- Funding: even borrowing the full £120 million takes leverage to about 2 times (a little more once the one-off costs and deal fees are paid), within the lenders' 3 times limit, but check there is still room under the limit if Hedgewick's profit fell
- Ask for warranties in the sale agreement (the sellers' promises about the accounts and contracts) and the right to lower the price if due diligence finds problems
- Upside not in the price: getting Hedgewick into more supermarkets through Thornbury's relationships

## 7. What a good recommendation includes

Must mention:
- Hedgewick is worth about £100 million on its own (10 x £10 million of EBITDA) and at most about £120 million to Thornbury including half of the £40 million synergy value, so Thornbury should not offer £150 million
- 40% of revenue comes from one supermarket with no long-term contract, and halving its orders would cut EBITDA to about £4 million and, on the bank's simple rule, the stand-alone value to about £40 million, so the offer needs protection against that risk
- A clear recommendation: an offer level, a walk-away price and next steps

Good signs:
- Separates the high-confidence savings (worth about £22 million) from the riskier crisp production move (about £18 million), and lowers the walk-away price if the production move looks doubtful (about £111 million on the high-confidence savings alone)
- Names the integration risks: Thornbury has never made crisps, gluten-free production in a wheat factory, the co-packer exit fee, and the sales founder leaving
- Sees that a £20 million earn-out protects only part of the £60 million downside from the largest customer, so the up-front price also depends on due diligence
- Points out that £40 million uses a first year with marketing unchanged: once marketing is cut the lasting fall is smaller (EBITDA of about £6.4 million, a value of about £64 million), though the multiple itself could fall
- Checks funding: even borrowing the full £120 million takes net debt to £160 million against £80 million of combined EBITDA, about 2 times (a little more once the one-off costs and deal fees are paid), within the lenders' 3 times limit
- Treats extra supermarket listings as upside that Thornbury should not pay for
- Uses the process: a non-binding first-round offer now, with the final price depending on due diligence on the largest customer and the factory move

## 8. Model answer outline

1. Clarify: Thornbury (about £700 million of revenue, £70 million EBITDA, mostly own-label) wants a fast-growing brand; the sellers hope for about £150 million; first-round offers are due in three weeks and are not binding. The bank values Hedgewick at 10 times this year's EBITDA, the sale is debt-free and cash-free so the price is the enterprise value, and the board will share at most half of the synergies.
2. Structure: Hedgewick on its own (growth, margins, quality of growth, stand-alone value), synergies (size, cost, certainty, timing), price versus the £150 million hoped for, risks (customer concentration, integration, brand), and the deal itself (funding, auction, protections).
3. Hedgewick on its own: revenue grew from £40 million to £60 million and EBITDA from £6 million to £10 million, with a steady 50% gross margin. At 10 times it is worth £100 million; £150 million is 15 times (the sellers' adviser calls it 12 times its own forecast of £12.5 million for next year, but that forecast relies on more growth at the largest customer). But 60% of the growth came from the largest customer, now 40% of revenue on a rolling agreement.
4. Synergies: £5 million a year once fully running, for £10 million of one-off costs, worth £50 million - £10 million = £40 million. The three high-confidence savings are worth about £22 million; the crisp production move, about £18 million, is the riskiest because Thornbury has never made crisps and must keep them gluten-free in a wheat factory.
5. Price: £100 million + half of £40 million = £120 million at most, £30 million below the sellers' hope. Even all the synergies would only reach £140 million. On the high-confidence savings alone, the most would be about £111 million.
6. Risk test: if the largest customer halved its orders, revenue would fall £12 million and EBITDA £6 million (to £4 million) in the first year, taking the stand-alone value to about £40 million on the bank's simple rule. Once marketing is cut back to 20% of sales, EBITDA would recover to about £6.4 million (about £64 million), though a brand that has lost shelf space might deserve a lower multiple. The production and buying savings would shrink too. The sales founder who manages that customer wants to leave at completion.
7. Recommendation: make a non-binding first-round offer of about £100 million paid up front (Hedgewick's stand-alone value), plus an earn-out of up to about £20 million paid later only if sales to the largest customer hold up after its next range review, with a walk-away price of £120 million, lowered to about £111 million if due diligence casts doubt on the crisp production move. Be clear about the limit: the £20 million paid later covers only a third of the £60 million the stand-alone value would lose on the bank's rule if that customer halved its orders, and the £100 million up front already assumes the customer holds. So the final, binding offer must depend on what due diligence finds about that customer. If Thornbury cannot get comfort, it should move more of the price into the later payment or lower the up-front amount, and accept that it may lose the auction.
8. Deal realities: the adviser will compare headline prices, so present the offer as up to £120 million; but that headline already equals the walk-away price, so the second round can change only the mix, not the total. The sellers may resist an earn-out tied to a customer whose relationship their sales founder will no longer manage, and an all-cash rival bid may look stronger, so Thornbury must be ready to lose rather than overpay.
9. Funding and next steps: even borrowing the full £120 million takes leverage to about 2 times (£160 million / £80 million), a little more once the one-off integration costs and deal fees are paid, still within the 3 times limit. Next steps for due diligence: test the largest customer relationship through a customer call arranged by the sellers' adviser, keep the sales founder through a handover, and check the crisp production plan in detail (the new line, the gluten-free room and the £7 million cost) before counting on that saving.
